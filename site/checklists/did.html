How to read a verdict

Pass and flag are decided by the stated conditions. A check that is out of scope for the paper is marked does not apply; that is never a verdict on what the paper said. When a check applies and the paper says nothing, the verdict is the one given under silent.

A verdict covers only the text the audit could see. A flag on material the paper says is in an appendix or replication package means "not shown here", not "not done", and such flags carry that caveat in the report. Thresholds are rules of thumb unless marked otherwise, and near-threshold values are reported as borderline rather than rounded to a verdict.

When a check applies

A check applies only when its scope trigger fires. Triggers key on the paper's own explicit stance and default to out of scope when the paper is silent.

all
Any paper that declares a difference-in-differences / two-way-fixed-effects design: it states it compares the change in an outcome for a treated group against the change for a control/comparison group across time. Fires on the paper's own naming of the design (DiD, difference-in-differences, TWFE, event study around a treatment date). Does NOT fire for a pure cross-sectional comparison or a single-group before/after with no comparison group.
covariate_adjusted
Fires only when the paper states it conditions its DiD estimate on covariates: it adds control variables to the regression, matches on covariates, invokes conditional parallel trends, or reweights on a propensity score. Near-miss that does NOT fire: mentioning covariates only to describe the sample; including unit and time fixed effects alone (those define the base DiD, not covariate adjustment). If the paper never states it adjusts for covariates, the trigger is not fired and the gated check is NA.
staggered_timing
Fires only when the paper states that its treated units begin treatment at different times (words the paper uses: staggered adoption, differential/variable treatment timing, rollout, cohorts first treated in different periods). Near-miss that does NOT fire: the paper states all treated units are treated at one common date (single-onset / block design); a two-period two-group design; or an event study built around one shared adoption date. If the paper never describes its own timing as varying across units, the trigger is not fired and the gated check is NA.
fuzzy_did
Fires only when the paper states its treatment is not a sharp 0/1 switch that all treated units share: it describes a fuzzy design, a treatment rate/intensity/dose that changes partially, a continuous or ordered or multi-valued treatment, or heterogeneous-adoption exposure. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a sharp binary treatment that some units switch on at different dates (that is staggered_timing, not fuzzy); describing treatment 'intensity' only in prose while estimating a binary indicator. If the paper never describes a non-sharp treatment, the trigger is not fired and the gated check is NA.
few_treated_clusters
Fires only when the paper states that a small number of units (or clusters) are treated or change treatment status, e.g. it reports one, or a handful of, treated states/regions/firms, or says few units switch. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a large treated group described with clustered standard errors; many treated units clustered into a few groups for inference only. If the paper does not state that few units are treated, the trigger is not fired and the gated check is NA.
repeated_cross_sections
Fires only when the paper states its data are repeated cross-sections, a fresh sample of individuals drawn each period rather than the same units followed over time. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a balanced or unbalanced panel of the same units; pooled panel data. If the paper does not describe its data as repeated cross-sections, the trigger is not fired and the gated check is NA.

Identification5

The assumptions the causal claim rests on.

DID-IDENT-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A DiD paper must state a no-anticipation assumption (or the anticipation horizon it allows).

In plain wordsThe paper should state that the treatment had no effect on treated units before it started, or say how far ahead of the start it allows effects to appear and defend that window.
Why it mattersThe method uses periods before treatment as a clean baseline, which only works if units were not already reacting to the coming treatment.
CheckWhether the paper states a no-anticipation assumption (or defines an anticipation horizon it will allow) for its treated units.
PassThe paper states that treatment has no effect before its onset, or explicitly specifies and defends an anticipation window it permits.
FlagThe paper estimates a DiD effect without stating any no-anticipation assumption or anticipation horizon.

DID-IDENT-02severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A DiD paper must acknowledge the no-interference / no-spillover (SUTVA) assumption its estimate relies on.

In plain wordsThe paper should acknowledge its no-spillover assumption (often called SUTVA), meaning treating some units does not change outcomes for others, or discuss whether such spillovers could occur.
Why it mattersThe method assumes each unit's outcome depends only on its own treatment, so spillovers or broader economy-wide effects would distort the estimate.
CheckWhether the paper acknowledges the no-interference / no-spillover (SUTVA) assumption, or discusses whether treatment of some units could affect the outcomes of others.
PassThe paper states the no-interference / SUTVA assumption, or explicitly discusses whether spillovers between treated and comparison units could contaminate the estimate.
FlagThe paper estimates a DiD effect without acknowledging the no-interference / no-spillover assumption at all.

DID-IDENT-03severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A DiD analysis must name its parallel/common-trends (or equivalent strict-exogeneity) identifying assumption; because that assumption is untestable, a paper that applies DiD without articulating it leaves its identification unstated. Offering a setting-specific reason to expect the assumption is recommended practice.

In plain wordsThe paper should name its key assumption, parallel trends (treated and comparison groups would have changed in the same way without treatment) or an equivalent; ideally it also explains why this is plausible in its setting.
Why it mattersThis assumption cannot be tested directly, so a paper that never states it leaves unclear what its causal claim rests on.
CheckWhether the paper names its parallel/common-trends (or strict-exogeneity) identifying assumption; as recommended practice it also offers a setting-specific reason to expect treated and comparison units would have moved together absent treatment.
PassThe paper states the parallel/common-trends (or strict-exogeneity) identifying assumption it relies on for identification.
FlagThe paper applies DiD without naming any parallel/common-trends or strict-exogeneity identifying assumption.

DID-IDENT-04severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: covariate_adjustedsilent: pass

A covariate-adjusted DiD must not condition on covariates measured after treatment onset or affected by the treatment.

In plain wordsWhen the paper adjusts for control variables, it should use only ones measured before treatment began or argued to be unaffected by the treatment, and treat any exception as a limitation.
Why it mattersControls are meant to make the comparison more credible, but adjusting for a variable the treatment itself can change biases the estimate.
CheckFor a paper that conditions on covariates, whether any control it uses is measured or realized after treatment onset (or is itself a plausible outcome of the treatment).
PassThe controls the paper conditions on are pre-treatment (measured before onset) or otherwise argued to be unaffected by the treatment.
FlagThe paper conditions its estimate on at least one covariate measured after treatment onset, or on a variable it acknowledges the treatment can affect, without treating that as a limitation.

DID-IDENT-05severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: covariate_adjustedsilent: flag

A covariate-adjusted DiD should report or discuss overlap (positivity) of the conditioning covariates across treated and comparison units.

In plain wordsWhen the paper adjusts for control variables, it should report or discuss overlap (also called common support): whether treated and comparison units are both present across the same values of those variables.
Why it mattersThe comparison group can only stand in for the treated group where both kinds of units exist at the same values of the controls.
CheckFor a paper conditioning on covariates, whether it reports or discusses common support / overlap of covariates between treated and comparison units (e.g. propensity scores bounded away from 0/1, a support/trimming discussion, a balance table over the conditioning set).
PassThe paper reports or discusses overlap of the conditioning covariates between treated and comparison groups (common support, bounded propensity scores, or an explicit overlap check).
FlagThe paper conditions on covariates for identification but reports no evidence or discussion of overlap / common support between treated and comparison units.

Estimation3

Whether the estimator recovers the target parameter.

DID-EST-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 8 sourcesapplies to: staggered_timingsilent: flag

A staggered-timing DiD must report a heterogeneity-robust estimate or state an ex-ante justification for assuming homogeneous treatment effects.

In plain wordsWhen units start treatment at different times, the paper should use an estimator built for effects that vary across groups and time, or argue in advance that effects are the same everywhere.
Why it mattersWith staggered timing and varying effects, the usual regression with unit and period fixed effects is biased: already-treated units act as comparisons, and some effects are even subtracted from the average rather than added.
CheckFor a paper that declares staggered timing, whether its main effect estimate comes from a heterogeneity-robust estimator (e.g. one that only compares newly-treated to not-yet-/never-treated units) OR is accompanied by a stated theoretical justification for assuming homogeneous effects.
PassThe paper reports a heterogeneity-robust estimate for its staggered design (a group-time / imputation / stacked / not-yet-treated-control estimator), or plain TWFE accompanied by an explicit ex-ante argument that treatment effects are homogeneous across groups and time.
FlagThe paper's staggered-timing effect rests on a single-coefficient or dynamic TWFE regression with no heterogeneity-robust estimate reported and no stated justification for a homogeneity restriction.
PrevalenceSeveral sources note the bias grows with the spread of adoption dates and shrinks with the share of never-treated units, so it is most severe in designs with wide timing variation and few never-treated units.
Further reading

  • Jonathan Roth, Pedro H. C. Sant’Anna, Alyssa Bilinski, & John Poe. (2023). What’s trending in difference-in-differences? A synthesis of the recent econometrics literature. Journal of Econometrics, 235(2), 2218-2244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2023.03.008, p. 2224
DID-EST-02severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: staggered_timingsilent: pass

Units already treated before the first observed period have no observed untreated baseline, so they cannot identify a treatment effect nor serve as a valid comparison group; a paper must not retain them as treated or control units as if that baseline existed.

In plain wordsThe paper should not keep units that were already treated before its data begin as treated or comparison units, as if their untreated outcomes had been observed.
Why it mattersThese units have no observed period without treatment, so they cannot show the treatment's effect or serve as a valid comparison.
CheckWhether the paper affirmatively retains units already treated before the first observed period as treated or comparison units as if their untreated baseline were observed.
PassThe paper does not retain units already treated before the first observed period as treated or comparison units. Designs with no such units comply; undisclosed pre-window treatment status defaults to PASS.
FlagThe paper affirmatively retains units already treated before the first observed period as treated or control units, with no note that their untreated outcomes are never observed.
Further reading
  • Brantly Callaway & Pedro H. C. Sant’Anna. (2020). Difference-in-Differences with multiple time periods. Journal of Econometrics, 225(2), 200-230. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2020.12.001, p. 204
DID-EST-03severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: fuzzy_didsilent: flag

A fuzzy / continuous-treatment DiD must state the treatment-effect homogeneity assumption behind its Wald-DiD estimate or identify a stable-exposure control group.

In plain wordsWhen treatment is partial, varies in intensity, or is adopted unevenly, the paper should state either that it assumes treatment effects are the same across units, or that its comparison group's exposure to treatment stays stable over time.
Why it mattersIn these designs the usual ratio estimate (called Wald-DiD) measures a causal effect only under such an equal-effects assumption, or with a comparison group whose exposure does not change.
CheckFor a paper that declares a non-sharp treatment, whether it states the additional assumption its fuzzy/continuous estimand needs, either a treatment-effect homogeneity condition or a control group with stable treatment exposure across periods.
PassThe paper states the homogeneity assumption behind its Wald-DiD estimate, or identifies a control group whose treatment exposure is stable over time for an assumption-free fuzzy estimand.
FlagThe paper estimates a fuzzy / continuous / heterogeneous-adoption DiD effect without stating either the required treatment-effect homogeneity assumption or a stable-exposure control group.
Further reading
  • Clément de Chaisemartin & Xavier D’Haultfœuille. (2017). Fuzzy Differences-in-Differences. The Review of Economic Studies, 85(2), 999-1028. https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdx049, pp. 999, 1001, 1005

Inference2

Standard errors, tests, and their validity.

DID-INFER-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A multi-period DiD must report inference that accounts for within-group serial correlation and clustering.

In plain wordsWith data over several periods, the paper should compute standard errors that allow outcomes within the same group to be correlated over time, for example by clustering at the group level.
Why it mattersOutcomes in the same group tend to move together over time, so treating each observation as independent makes estimates look more precise than they are.
CheckWhether the paper's reported inference accounts for within-group correlation, e.g. standard errors clustered at the group level, a block bootstrap, aggregation to pre/post, or another serial-correlation-robust procedure, rather than assuming independent errors.
PassThe paper reports standard errors that account for within-group serial correlation and clustering (cluster-robust at the assignment level, block bootstrap, pre/post aggregation, or an equivalent serial-correlation-robust method).
FlagThe paper reports conventional/independent-observation standard errors (or does not describe how it handles serial correlation and clustering) for a multi-period DiD.
Further reading
  • M. Bertrand, Esther Duflo, & Sendhil Mullainathan. (2004). How Much Should We Trust Differences-In-Differences Estimates?. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 119(1), 249-275. https://doi.org/10.1162/003355304772839588, pp. 249-250
  • Coady Wing, Kosali Simon, & Ricardo A. Bello-Gómez. (2018). Designing Difference in Difference Studies: Best Practices for Public Health Policy Research. Annual Review of Public Health, 39(1), 453-469. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-publhealth-040617-013507, p. 462
DID-INFER-02severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: few_treated_clusterssilent: flag

A DiD with few treated units/clusters must use inference designed for the small-cluster case rather than routine large-sample cluster-robust standard errors.

In plain wordsWhen only a few units or clusters are treated, the paper should measure uncertainty with a method designed for that case, such as a permutation test, rather than standard clustered standard errors.
Why it mattersWith few treated units, standard clustered standard errors understate uncertainty and find effects too often.
CheckFor a paper that states it has few treated units/clusters, whether it uses an inference method suited to that case (randomization/permutation or Conley-Taber-type inference, pre/post aggregation with a small-sample-adjusted threshold, a wild-cluster bootstrap with stated caveats, or reported wider intervals) rather than routine large-sample cluster-robust standard errors.
PassThe paper uses a small-cluster-appropriate inference procedure for its few treated units (randomization/permutation, Conley-Taber, aggregated inference with adjusted critical values, or an explicitly caveated wild bootstrap).
FlagThe paper has few treated units/clusters yet reports only routine large-sample cluster-robust standard errors, with no small-sample adjustment or alternative method.

Robustness4

Sensitivity, diagnostics, and alternative specifications.

DID-ROBUST-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A DiD paper should report at least one of the enumerated parallel-trends robustness artifacts (formal sensitivity analysis / robust confidence set, placebo or pre-period test, alternative comparison group, or a trend-robustness specification check).

In plain wordsThe paper should report at least one check on the parallel-trends assumption (that groups would have moved together without treatment): a sensitivity analysis, a placebo or pre-treatment test, another comparison group, or an alternative specification.
Why it mattersBecause the assumption cannot be tested and pre-treatment checks can miss real violations, usual confidence intervals can mislead; probing sensitivity shows how much the conclusion depends on it.
CheckWhether the paper reports at least one item from an enumerated set of parallel-trends robustness artifacts: (a) a formal parallel-trends sensitivity analysis or robust confidence set (e.g. one that bounds the post-treatment trend violation by the pre-treatment violation); (b) a placebo or pre-period test aimed at detecting trend violations; (c) estimates under at least one alternative comparison/control group; or (d) estimates under at least one alternative specification that the paper presents as a check on the trend assumption. Presence of any one item is readable from the paper's tables, figures, or text.
PassThe paper reports at least one of the enumerated artifacts (a)–(d).
FlagThe paper reports its DiD estimate and none of the enumerated artifacts (a)–(d) appears anywhere in the paper, appendix, or supplement.
Further reading
  • Ashesh Rambachan & Jonathan Roth. (2023). A More Credible Approach to Parallel Trends. The Review of Economic Studies, 90(5), 2555-2591. https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdad018, pp. 2556, 2559
  • Jonathan Roth, Pedro H. C. Sant’Anna, Alyssa Bilinski, & John Poe. (2023). What’s trending in difference-in-differences? A synthesis of the recent econometrics literature. Journal of Econometrics, 235(2), 2218-2244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2023.03.008, p. 2234
DID-ROBUST-02severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A DiD analysis should present pre-treatment trend evidence bearing on parallel trends -- an event-study/leads plot, pre-period effect estimates, or plotted group means -- since visibly divergent pre-trends make the estimate suspect; such evidence is necessary but not conclusive (pre-trend tests can be underpowered and pre-period parallelism does not guarantee post-period parallelism).

In plain wordsThe paper should show how treated and comparison groups moved before treatment, for example with an event-study plot, pre-treatment effect estimates, or plotted group averages.
Why it mattersClearly different trends before treatment make the estimate suspect, though similar trends do not prove the groups would have kept moving together afterward.
CheckWhether the paper presents pre-treatment trend evidence: an event-study/leads plot, pre-period (placebo) effect estimates, or plotted group means over pre-periods.
PassThe paper shows pre-treatment trend evidence bearing on parallel trends.
FlagThe paper reports a DiD estimate with no pre-treatment trend evidence of any kind.

DID-ROBUST-03severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: pass

A DiD paper must not treat passed pre-trends as proof of parallel trends, nor condition its analysis on the outcome of a pre-trends test.

In plain wordsThe paper should not claim that similar trends before treatment prove the parallel-trends assumption, and should not choose its sample, specification, or whether to proceed based on passing a pre-trends test.
Why it mattersSimilar earlier trends do not guarantee similar later ones, such tests often miss real differences, and choosing an analysis because it passed one biases the results.
CheckWhether the paper (a) claims that observing parallel pre-trends establishes the parallel-trends assumption, or (b) states it selected its sample, specification, or decision to proceed on the basis of passing a pre-trends test.
PassThe paper does not treat parallel pre-trends as proof of the assumption and does not condition its analysis on passing a pre-trends test (silence about pre-testing counts as compliance here).
FlagThe paper asserts that parallel pre-trends establish the parallel-trends assumption, or states it retained/dropped the design or filtered results based on the outcome of a pre-trends test.

DID-ROBUST-04severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 6 sourcesapplies to: staggered_timingsilent: pass

A staggered-timing DiD must not use plain TWFE event-study lead coefficients as its test of pre-trends.

In plain wordsWhen units start treatment at different times, the paper should not rely on the pre-treatment ("lead") coefficients from a standard two-way fixed effects event study as its test of pre-trends.
Why it mattersWith staggered timing and varying effects, those coefficients mix in treatment effects from other periods and groups, so they are not a valid pre-trends test.
CheckFor a paper that declares staggered timing, whether the pre-trends evidence it relies on comes from the lead coefficients of a plain TWFE event-study regression, rather than from a heterogeneity-robust pre-trends test (leads from a robust group-time/imputation estimator, or a test run on untreated observations only).
PassThe paper either uses a heterogeneity-robust pre-trends test for its staggered design, or does not present TWFE event-study lead coefficients as evidence for parallel trends.
FlagThe paper relies on the lead coefficients of a plain TWFE event-study regression as its pre-trends test in a staggered-timing design.

Reporting1

What the paper must state for a reader to evaluate it.

DID-REPORT-01severity if flagged: lowmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A DiD paper should state its target estimand and the comparison groups and time frame that identify it.

In plain wordsThe paper should name the quantity it aims to estimate, such as the average effect on the treated units, and say which comparison groups and time periods it uses.
Why it mattersLeaving the target quantity implicit in a regression setup is a central source of problems in these designs, so the target and comparison should be explicit.
CheckWhether the paper states its target estimand (e.g. the ATT or an event-study path) and identifies the comparison group(s) and time frame used to identify it.
PassThe paper names its target estimand and identifies the comparison group(s) and time frame that identify it.
FlagThe paper reports a DiD coefficient without stating the estimand it targets or the comparison group(s) identifying it.
If flagged, the author can fix it byState the target estimand you are after (e.g. the ATT, or an event-study path of effects) and which units and periods identify it, the comparison group and the time frame. Making the estimand and the identifying comparison explicit, rather than letting a regression specification implicitly impose them, lets a reader see what your coefficient represents.

DID-DATA-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: repeated_cross_sectionssilent: flag

A repeated-cross-section DiD must address the stability of group composition and covariate distribution across periods.

In plain wordsWhen the paper uses different samples in each period (repeated cross-sections), it should address whether the makeup of each group and its characteristics stay stable across periods.
Why it mattersIf the kinds of units in the groups shift over time, those shifts can bias the estimate.
CheckFor a paper stating it uses repeated cross-sections, whether it addresses the stability of group composition and covariate distribution across periods (states the stable-composition assumption, or reports evidence that composition does not shift with treatment).
PassThe paper states or examines that group membership and the covariate distribution are stable across periods (no compositional change confounding the comparison).
FlagThe paper uses repeated cross-sections but never addresses whether group composition or the covariate distribution shifts across periods.
