The checklists

Panel data

11 checks, each resting on claims from the methods literature. The 14 sources listed at the end are the ones cited as further reading. Each check says when it applies, what passes, what is flagged, and where to read more.

How to read a verdict

Pass and flag are decided by the stated conditions. A check that is out of scope for the paper is marked does not apply; that is never a verdict on what the paper said. When a check applies and the paper says nothing, the verdict is the one given under silent.

A verdict covers only the text the audit could see. A flag on material the paper says is in an appendix or replication package means "not shown here", not "not done", and such flags carry that caveat in the report. Thresholds are rules of thumb unless marked otherwise, and near-threshold values are reported as borderline rather than rounded to a verdict.

When a check applies

A check applies only when its scope trigger fires. Triggers key on the paper's own explicit stance and default to out of scope when the paper is silent.

did
Does the paper explicitly state that it uses a difference-in-differences design or a two-way (unit and period) fixed-effects design to estimate a treatment/exposure effect by contrasting treated and untreated units over time? Fires on that declared design. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a cross-sectional treated-vs-untreated comparison with no over-time contrast, or descriptive within-unit trends with no treatment/control group. When it is unclear whether a treatment/control over-time contrast is being drawn, the check is NA.
self_selected_treatment
Does the paper explicitly state that treatment/exposure status is adopted or chosen by the units themselves (observational, not randomized or as-if-random)? Fires on a declared self-selected/observational treatment. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a randomized or natural-experiment/as-if-random assignment the paper claims exogenous. Ambiguous cases resolve OUT (NA).
dynamic_panel
Does the paper explicitly state that its specification includes a lagged dependent variable or otherwise models autoregressive dynamics in the outcome? Fires on a declared dynamic/autoregressive panel model. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a static panel model whose only lags are lagged covariates (no lagged outcome), or serial-correlation-robust standard errors with no lagged-outcome term. Ambiguous cases resolve OUT (NA).
nonlinear_fe
Does the paper explicitly state that it estimates a nonlinear or limited-dependent-variable model (for example a binary probit or logit, or another discrete-choice model) that includes individual fixed effects? Fires on that declared model class. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a linear fixed-effects regression, or a pooled logit/probit with no individual effects. Ambiguous cases resolve OUT (NA).
random_effects
Does the paper explicitly state that it estimates a random-effects / error-components model, treating the individual effects as uncorrelated with the regressors? Fires on that declared estimator. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a fixed-effects/within estimator, or clustered standard errors on a pooled regression. Ambiguous cases resolve OUT (NA).
panel_tscs
Does the paper explicitly state that it observes the same units over two or more time periods and estimates a model across those periods? Fires on a self-description of repeated-over-time observations on fixed units. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a single cross-section, or pooled/repeated cross-sections in which different units are sampled each period without following the same units. If it is genuinely unclear whether the same units are tracked over time, the check is NA, not FLAG.
dynamic_gmm
Does the paper explicitly state that it estimates a dynamic panel model by GMM or IV using lagged levels and/or lagged differences of the variables as instruments (Anderson-Hsiao / Arellano-Bond difference-GMM or Blundell-Bond system-GMM)? Fires on the declared use of that estimator family. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a static FE regression, or lagged regressors used as ordinary controls without instrumenting a lagged outcome. Ambiguous cases resolve OUT (NA).
factor_model
Fires when the paper states it estimates an unobserved-common-factor or interactive-fixed-effects panel model (principal-components factors, CCE / common-correlated-effects, interactive effects). Near-miss that does NOT fire: additive two-way fixed effects, or a single common linear time trend, which are not a declared factor structure.
macro_panel
Does the paper explicitly describe its data as a panel with both a large cross-section and a long time series of comparable magnitude (for example countries, regions, or industries observed over many periods)? Fires on that self-description. Near-miss that does NOT fire: a short micro-panel (many units, only a handful of periods), which the paper describes as small-T. Ambiguous cases resolve OUT (NA).
attrition_panel
Fires when the paper states its panel is unbalanced or subject to attrition/dropout across waves (units present in some waves but absent in others). Near-miss that does NOT fire: a balanced panel with complete waves for every unit; item nonresponse on a single covariate within otherwise-complete waves.

Identification2

The assumptions the causal claim rests on.

PANEL-IDENT-01severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: didsilent: flag

A paper using a difference-in-differences or two-way fixed-effects design should provide evidence supporting the assumption that treated and untreated units share a common counterfactual outcome trend.

In plain wordsA paper using a difference-in-differences or two-way fixed-effects design (comparing changes over time between treated and untreated units) should show evidence that both groups' outcomes moved in parallel before treatment, such as a pre-period trend comparison, an event-study plot, or a placebo test on leads.
Why it mattersThe estimate is credible only if treated and untreated units would have followed the same outcome path without treatment.
CheckWhether the paper presents evidence on pre-treatment outcome trends for treated versus untreated units (a pre-period trend comparison, an event-study/leads-and-lags plot, or a placebo lead test).
PassThe paper reports a comparison of treated and untreated pre-treatment outcome trajectories, or a leads-and-lags / event-study specification whose pre-treatment coefficients it examines, as support for the parallel-counterfactual premise.
FlagThe paper estimates a difference-in-differences or two-way fixed-effects treatment effect but presents no pre-treatment trend comparison, event-study, or lead/placebo evidence anywhere in the text, appendices, or supplement.
Further reading
  • Julia Payson. (2019). Cities in the Statehouse: How Local Governments Use Lobbyists to Secure State Funding. The Journal of Politics, 82(2), 403-417. https://doi.org/10.1086/706767, pp. 409-410
  • Badi H. Baltagi. (2021). Econometric Analysis of Panel Data. Springer texts in business and economics. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-53953-5, p. 23
PANEL-IDENT-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: self_selected_treatmentsilent: flag

A paper with self-selected treatment should include an explicit statement restricting its estimand to the treated/selecting units rather than leaving it as a generic or population-average effect.

In plain wordsWhen units choose whether to receive treatment, the paper should state explicitly that its estimate is the effect for the units that took up treatment, rather than a general effect for everyone.
Why it mattersWith self-selected treatment, the design identifies an effect only for the units that took it up, so reading it as a population-wide effect goes beyond what the design supports.
CheckWhether the paper includes an explicit statement restricting its reported estimand to the units that receive/select into treatment (an effect-on-the-treated or named-subpopulation quantity), as opposed to no such restriction.
PassThe paper explicitly states that its estimate is the effect for the units that select into (or receive) treatment -- an effect-on-the-treated or named-subpopulation quantity -- and interprets its conclusions on that basis.
FlagThe paper has self-selected treatment and reports a treatment/exposure effect but contains no explicit statement restricting the estimand to the treated/selecting subpopulation (it leaves the estimate as a generic causal effect).
Further reading
  • Manuel Arellano & Stéphane Bonhomme. (2011). Identifying Distributional Characteristics in Random Coefficients Panel Data Models. The Review of Economic Studies, 79(3), 987-1020. https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdr045, p. 991
  • Julia Payson. (2019). Cities in the Statehouse: How Local Governments Use Lobbyists to Secure State Funding. The Journal of Politics, 82(2), 403-417. https://doi.org/10.1086/706767, p. 411

Estimation3

Whether the estimator recovers the target parameter.

PANEL-EST-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: dynamic_panelsilent: flag

A dynamic (lagged-outcome) panel paper should either estimate the autoregressive coefficient with a bias-aware method or make an explicit argument that its time dimension is long enough that within-groups bias is negligible.

In plain wordsA panel model that uses the previous period's outcome as a predictor should estimate that coefficient with a method built to avoid short-panel bias (such as GMM with lagged instruments or a bias correction), or argue that the panel has enough time periods for the bias to be negligible.
Why it mattersWith few time periods, ordinary least squares and fixed-effects estimates of that coefficient are biased.
CheckWhich estimator the paper uses for the lagged-dependent-variable coefficient, and whether it addresses the short-panel dynamic bias (via GMM/IV, a bias-corrected or LIML-type estimator, or an explicit large-T argument).
PassThe paper estimates the lagged-outcome coefficient with a method that addresses dynamic-panel bias (GMM/IV on lagged instruments, an analytic bias correction, or a LIML-type estimator), OR explicitly argues that its time dimension is long enough for within-groups bias to be negligible.
FlagThe paper reports a lagged-dependent-variable coefficient estimated by plain OLS or plain within-groups/fixed-effects, with neither a bias-aware estimator NOR an explicit large-T (long-time-series) justification anywhere in the text, appendices, or supplement.
Further reading
  • T. W. Anderson & Chêng Hsiao. (1982). Formulation and estimation of dynamic models using panel data. Journal of Econometrics, 18(1), 47-82. https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-4076(82)90095-1, p. 81
  • Manuel Arellano & Stephen Bond. (1991). Some Tests of Specification for Panel Data: Monte Carlo Evidence and an Application to Employment Equations. The Review of Economic Studies, 58(2), 277. https://doi.org/10.2307/2297968, p. 285
  • Richard Blundell & Stephen Bond. (1998). Initial conditions and moment restrictions in dynamic panel data models. Journal of Econometrics, 87(1), 115-143. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0304-4076(98)00009-8, p. 128
  • Javier Álvarez & Manuel Arellano. (2003). The Time Series and Cross-Section Asymptotics of Dynamic Panel Data Estimators. Econometrica, 71(4), 1121-1159. https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00441, p. 1121
PANEL-EST-02severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: nonlinear_fesilent: flag

A nonlinear fixed-effects panel paper should estimate by conditional likelihood or an incidental-parameters bias correction, or else explicitly justify a time dimension long enough that the incidental-parameters bias is negligible.

In plain wordsA nonlinear fixed-effects model, such as a logit or probit with a separate effect for each unit, should use conditional likelihood or a bias correction, or the paper should argue it has enough time periods for the bias to be negligible.
Why it mattersEstimating every unit's effect directly from few time periods gives inconsistent results, a flaw known as the incidental-parameters problem.
CheckWhether a nonlinear fixed-effects model on a short panel is estimated by a conditional-likelihood / sufficiency approach or an incidental-parameters bias correction, rather than by unconditional maximum likelihood that estimates every individual effect directly.
PassThe paper estimates its nonlinear fixed-effects model with a conditional-likelihood estimator, a bias-corrected (analytic or jackknife) estimator, or another named method it identifies as handling the incidental-parameters problem, OR explicitly argues its time dimension is long enough for the bias to be negligible.
FlagThe paper estimates a nonlinear fixed-effects model by unconditional maximum likelihood that estimates each individual effect directly, with neither a conditional-likelihood/incidental-parameters bias correction NOR an explicit large-T justification.
Further reading
  • Gary Chamberlain. (1984). Chapter 22 Panel data. Handbook of econometrics, 1247-1318. https://doi.org/10.1016/s1573-4412(84)02014-6, p. 1250
  • Jinyong Hahn & Whitney K. Newey. (2004). Jackknife and Analytical Bias Reduction for Nonlinear Panel Models. Econometrica, 72(4), 1295-1319. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00533.x, p. 1295
  • Bo E. Honoré & Ekaterini Kyriazidou. (2000). Panel Data Discrete Choice Models with Lagged Dependent Variables. Econometrica, 68(4), 839-874. https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00139, p. 859
PANEL-EST-03severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: random_effectssilent: flag

A random-effects panel paper should justify, by a specification test or explicit argument, that its regressors are uncorrelated with the individual effects.

In plain wordsA paper using random effects should justify, with a test such as a Hausman test comparing fixed and random effects or with an explicit argument, that its predictors are unrelated to the unit-specific effects.
Why it mattersRandom-effects estimates are valid only if the predictors are unrelated to those unit-specific effects.
CheckWhether a random-effects paper reports a specification test (for example a Hausman test comparing fixed and random effects) or an explicit argument for why the regressors are uncorrelated with the individual effects.
PassThe paper reports a Hausman-type comparison of fixed versus random effects, or another test/argument, supporting the assumption that its regressors are uncorrelated with the individual effects before using the random-effects estimates.
FlagThe paper reports random-effects estimates without any test or explicit justification of the assumption that the regressors are uncorrelated with the individual effects.
Further reading
  • Badi H. Baltagi. (2021). Econometric Analysis of Panel Data. Springer texts in business and economics. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-53953-5, p. 29

Inference2

Standard errors, tests, and their validity.

PANEL-INFER-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: panel_tscssilent: flag

A panel/time-series-cross-section paper should report standard errors robust to within-unit serial correlation, typically by clustering at the unit level or above.

In plain wordsA panel paper should report standard errors that allow errors to be correlated over time within the same unit, typically by clustering at the unit level or higher.
Why it mattersRepeated observations on the same unit tend to be correlated, so standard errors that assume independence give misleading test results.
CheckWhether the reported standard errors are clustered at the unit level or above, or otherwise explicitly made robust to within-unit serial correlation, as opposed to default OLS / independence-assuming standard errors.
PassThe paper reports standard errors clustered at the unit (or higher) level, or another correction it identifies as accommodating within-unit serial correlation of the errors.
FlagThe paper reports default or independence-assuming standard errors on panel data with no clustering and no other stated correction for within-unit serial correlation.
Further reading
  • Christian Hansen. (2006). Asymptotic properties of a robust variance matrix estimator for panel data when is large. Journal of Econometrics, 141(2), 597-620. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jeconom.2006.10.009, pp. 598, 611
  • Badi H. Baltagi. (2021). Econometric Analysis of Panel Data. Springer texts in business and economics. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-53953-5, p. 22
  • Tom O’Grady. (2017). How do Economic Circumstances Determine Preferences? Evidence from Long-run Panel Data. British Journal of Political Science, 49(4), 1381-1406. https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007123417000242, p. 1393
  • Julia Payson. (2019). Cities in the Statehouse: How Local Governments Use Lobbyists to Secure State Funding. The Journal of Politics, 82(2), 403-417. https://doi.org/10.1086/706767, p. 409
PANEL-INFER-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: dynamic_gmmsilent: flag

A dynamic-panel GMM paper reporting two-step estimates should correct the two-step standard errors for finite-sample bias or report one-step inference.

In plain wordsA dynamic-panel paper reporting two-step GMM (generalized method of moments) estimates should base its inference on standard errors with a finite-sample correction or on one-step estimates.
Why it mattersUncorrected two-step standard errors understate the true uncertainty in the estimates, so tests reject too often.
CheckWhether a paper reporting two-step GMM estimates uses a finite-sample standard-error correction, or reports inference from the one-step estimator, rather than relying on uncorrected two-step asymptotic standard errors.
PassThe paper draws its inference either from one-step GMM or from two-step GMM with a stated finite-sample correction to the standard errors.
FlagThe paper reports two-step GMM estimates and bases its inference on the uncorrected two-step asymptotic standard errors, with no finite-sample correction and no one-step results.
Further reading
  • Richard Blundell & Stephen Bond. (1998). Initial conditions and moment restrictions in dynamic panel data models. Journal of Econometrics, 87(1), 115-143. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0304-4076(98)00009-8, pp. 139, 142
  • Manuel Arellano & Stephen Bond. (1991). Some Tests of Specification for Panel Data: Monte Carlo Evidence and an Application to Employment Equations. The Review of Economic Studies, 58(2), 277. https://doi.org/10.2307/2297968, p. 285

Robustness3

Sensitivity, diagnostics, and alternative specifications.

PANEL-ROBUST-01severity if flagged: highlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: dynamic_gmmsilent: flag

A dynamic-panel GMM paper should report a test for serial correlation in the residuals (the second-order autocorrelation test) that validates its instruments.

In plain wordsA dynamic-panel GMM paper should report a test for second-order serial correlation in its differenced residuals, which checks whether its instruments are valid.
Why it mattersThe lagged values used as instruments are valid only if the errors are not correlated over time at that order.
CheckWhether a dynamic-panel GMM paper reports a test for serial correlation in the (differenced) residuals -- the second-order autocorrelation / instrument-validity test -- alongside its estimates.
PassThe paper reports a test for (absence of) second-order serial correlation in the differenced residuals, or an equivalent instrument-validity serial-correlation test, together with the GMM estimates.
FlagThe paper reports dynamic-panel GMM estimates with no serial-correlation / second-order autocorrelation test of the instruments' validity anywhere in the text, appendices, or supplement.
Further reading
  • Manuel Arellano & Stephen Bond. (1991). Some Tests of Specification for Panel Data: Monte Carlo Evidence and an Application to Employment Equations. The Review of Economic Studies, 58(2), 277. https://doi.org/10.2307/2297968, pp. 278, 281
PANEL-ROBUST-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: factor_modelsilent: flag

A factor / interactive-effects paper must determine the number of factors from the data rather than assume it.

In plain wordsA factor or interactive-effects model (one with unobserved common factors that affect units differently) should choose the number of factors with a data-driven rule, such as an information criterion with a stated maximum, or show that results hold across factor counts.
Why it mattersThe model is valid only with the correct number of factors, so simply assuming a count is not enough.
CheckWhether the paper reports how the number of common factors was chosen, a data-driven selection procedure (information criterion, eigenvalue-ratio, or equivalent) with its search bound, versus asserting a factor count without justification.
PassThe paper reports a data-driven procedure for selecting the number of factors (an information criterion or equivalent selection rule, with a stated maximum candidate count) or reports robustness of results to the factor count.
FlagThe paper fixes the number of factors by assumption with no data-driven selection or robustness check.
Further reading
  • Jushan Bai & Serena Ng. (2002). Determining the Number of Factors in Approximate Factor Models. Econometrica, 70(1), 191-221. https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00273, pp. 191, 209
PANEL-ROBUST-03severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: macro_panelsilent: flag

A macro-panel paper (large cross-section and long time series) should report a cross-sectional-dependence test on its residuals, or adopt an estimator/standard-error correction it states accounts for cross-unit error dependence.

In plain wordsA panel with many units and many time periods should test its residuals for correlation across units, or use an estimator or standard-error correction that it states accounts for such correlation.
Why it mattersErrors correlated across units can bias estimates and make tests unreliable.
CheckWhether a macro-panel paper reports a test for cross-sectional (cross-unit) error dependence, or uses an estimator/correction it identifies as accommodating such dependence.
PassThe paper reports a cross-sectional-dependence test on its residuals, or adopts an estimator/standard-error correction it states accounts for cross-unit error dependence.
FlagThe paper analyzes a large-N large-T macro panel with no cross-sectional-dependence test and no estimator or correction addressing cross-unit error dependence.
Further reading
  • M. Hashem Pesaran. (2006). Estimation and Inference in Large Heterogeneous Panels with a Multifactor Error Structure. Econometrica, 74(4), 967-1012. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2006.00692.x, p. 970
  • Badi H. Baltagi. (2021). Econometric Analysis of Panel Data. Springer texts in business and economics. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-53953-5, p. 12

Data1

Sample construction and measurement.

PANEL-DATA-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: attrition_panelsilent: flag

A panel paper with attrition/dropout must assess whether the attrition is selective.

In plain wordsA panel that loses units over time should assess whether dropout is selective, for example by comparing retained units with dropped units on key characteristics, or by correcting for dropout in a model.
Why it mattersSelective dropout is the most damaging threat to the validity of panel results.
CheckWhether the paper reports an assessment of selective attrition, for example, comparing the retained analysis sample to units observed in fewer / only one wave on key variables.
PassThe paper reports an attrition assessment, such as comparing retained units to dropped / once-observed units on key characteristics, or a model-based correction for selective dropout.
FlagThe paper's panel is unbalanced / subject to attrition but it reports no assessment of whether the dropout is selective.
Further reading
  • Badi H. Baltagi. (2021). Econometric Analysis of Panel Data. Springer texts in business and economics. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-53953-5, p. 11
  • Tom O’Grady. (2017). How do Economic Circumstances Determine Preferences? Evidence from Long-run Panel Data. British Journal of Political Science, 49(4), 1381-1406. https://doi.org/10.1017/s0007123417000242, p. 1391
