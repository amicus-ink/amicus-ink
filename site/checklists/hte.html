The checklists

Heterogeneous treatment effects

24 checks, each resting on claims from the methods literature. The 19 sources listed at the end are the ones cited as further reading. Each check says when it applies, what passes, what is flagged, and where to read more.

How to read a verdict

Pass and flag are decided by the stated conditions. A check that is out of scope for the paper is marked does not apply; that is never a verdict on what the paper said. When a check applies and the paper says nothing, the verdict is the one given under silent.

A verdict covers only the text the audit could see. A flag on material the paper says is in an appendix or replication package means "not shown here", not "not done", and such flags carry that caveat in the report. Thresholds are rules of thumb unless marked otherwise, and near-threshold values are reported as borderline rather than rounded to a verdict.

When a check applies

A check applies only when its scope trigger fires. Triggers key on the paper's own explicit stance and default to out of scope when the paper is silent.

all
Every paper in the module (all are causal / potential-outcomes analyses of treatment effects). Fires unconditionally.
observational
The paper states that its treatment-effect estimates come from non-randomized data and relies on a selection-on-observables / unconfoundedness identifying assumption. Does NOT fire for: a randomized experiment (even one that also runs adjusted regressions), or a design whose identification the paper attributes to randomization or an instrument.
iv
The paper states it identifies effects through an instrumental variable, or a fuzzy regression-discontinuity estimated by two-stage least squares. Does NOT fire when: the paper merely calls a policy an 'instrument' informally with no IV identification claim; a variable is used only as a control; standard errors happen to be clustered.
generalization
The paper states it carries an estimate from its study sample to a different or broader target population (population effect, transportability, external target). Does NOT fire for: a passing remark about external validity; reporting subgroup effects that stay within the study sample.
interaction_model
The paper states it estimates a multiplicative interaction / product term (treatment x moderator, or X x Z) to test a conditional or moderating hypothesis. Does NOT fire for: adding a covariate as a control with no product term; reporting means by subgroup with no product term; a stratified analysis with no interaction coefficient.
nonlinear_interaction
interaction_model AND the outcome model is a nonlinear / limited-dependent model the paper names (logit, probit, tobit, Poisson or other count, duration/hazard, ordered, multinomial). Does NOT fire for: a linear/OLS interaction; a nonlinear model that contains no product or higher-order term.
multiple_hypotheses
The paper states it tests a family of several hypotheses at once -- treatment effects on multiple outcomes, or across multiple subgroups/treatments -- and reports the tests together. Does NOT fire for: a single pre-specified primary outcome/hypothesis; incidental coefficients from one model that the paper does not treat as a tested family.
heterogeneity_test
Fires when the paper states it performs a statistical test for the presence of effect variation (an omnibus or constant-effect test, a variance-ratio test, a shifted-CDF/KS test, or a randomization test of no heterogeneity). Near-miss that does NOT fire: comparing two subgroup point estimates with no test; a test of the overall average effect.
causal_forest
The paper states it uses a random / causal / regression forest to estimate treatment effects and to build confidence intervals. Does NOT fire for: a single descriptive regression tree; a random-effects (multilevel) model; a forest used only for propensity or nuisance prediction that the paper does not use for effect inference.
quantile_distributional
The paper states a quantile treatment effect or a distributional / CDF treatment effect as an estimand it reports. Does NOT fire for: reporting subgroup means; a single median regression the paper does not frame as a distributional-effect claim.
clustered
The paper states its observations are clustered or otherwise dependent (e.g., pupils within classrooms, siblings, repeated measures on a unit) and describes that grouping. Does NOT fire for: a single cross-section the paper treats as independent units; time fixed effects alone.
subgroup_analysis
Fires when the paper states it reports or tests treatment effects for subgroups defined by covariates. Near-miss that does NOT fire: reporting only the overall average effect; adjusting for a covariate without any subgroup-specific effect.
data_driven_hte
Fires when the paper states it uses a data-adaptive/machine-learning method to discover or estimate heterogeneity (causal forest, penalized/LASSO selection, ensemble/super-learner, causal or recursive-partitioning trees, BART). Near-miss that does NOT fire: pre-registered fixed subgroups fit by OLS.
linear_interaction
interaction_model estimated by a linear (OLS-type) specification in which at least one interacted variable is continuous. Does NOT fire for: a fully saturated dummy-by-dummy model with no continuous interacted variable; a nonlinear-link model.
subgroup_heterogeneity
The paper states that variation of the treatment effect across subpopulations (heterogeneous / conditional / effect-modified effects) is a quantity it targets and reports. Does NOT fire for: a single robustness split the paper does not frame as a heterogeneity estimand; reporting only an overall average effect.
risk_modeling
The paper states it stratifies or predicts treatment effects using a baseline-risk / prognostic model or risk score. Does NOT fire for: one-variable-at-a-time subgroup analysis; adjusting for baseline covariates without forming a risk score.
trimming
The paper states it discards or trims units by their estimated propensity score, or restricts the analysis to a common-support / overlap region. Does NOT fire for: dropping units for item-missing data; winsorizing or trimming the outcome variable.
attrition
The paper states it has non-trivial follow-up attrition or missing outcome data. Does NOT fire for: complete outcome data; missingness confined to covariates.

Identification6

The assumptions the causal claim rests on.

HTE-IDENT-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

The paper should state the no-interference / SUTVA assumption its effect estimates rely on, or show how the design rules interference out.

In plain wordsThe paper should state that each unit's outcome depends only on its own treatment, not on other units' treatment (the no-interference or SUTVA assumption), or show how its design rules out such spillover.
Why it mattersEffect estimates rely on this assumption; if one unit's treatment can affect another's outcome, as within a household, the analysis has to move to a level where no spillover occurs.
CheckWhether the paper states a no-interference or SUTVA assumption, or otherwise addresses spillover between units (including by choosing the unit of analysis to rule it out).
PassThe paper names a no-interference / SUTVA assumption, or explains how its unit of analysis or design removes cross-unit spillover.
FlagThe paper reports treatment-effect estimates with no statement of a no-interference / SUTVA assumption and no treatment of possible spillover between units.

A paper identifying effects from observational data should state its unconfoundedness / selection-on-observables assumption.

In plain wordsWhen effects come from non-randomized data, the paper should state its unconfoundedness assumption: once the measured covariates are accounted for, no unmeasured factor drives both treatment and outcome.
Why it mattersWithout random assignment this assumption does not hold automatically, so the result depends on it.
CheckWhether the paper states an unconfoundedness / strong-ignorability / no-unmeasured-confounders assumption for its observational identification.
PassThe paper names an unconfoundedness / conditional-ignorability / no-unmeasured-confounders condition as the basis for its observational effect estimates.
FlagThe paper estimates effects from non-randomized data but states no conditional-independence / unconfoundedness assumption.

HTE-IDENT-03severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: observationalsilent: flag

A paper estimating conditional effects from observational data should state or check overlap / common support.

In plain wordsWhen it estimates covariate-specific effects from non-randomized data, the paper should state or check overlap (common support), meaning that units with similar covariates appear in both the treated and untreated groups.
Why it mattersWhere some covariate values have few treated or few untreated units, estimates become biased, imprecise, and sensitive to modeling choices.
CheckWhether the paper states an overlap / common-support condition or reports checking/handling limited overlap in the covariate distribution across treatment arms.
PassThe paper states an overlap / common-support assumption or reports a diagnostic or restriction addressing limited overlap.
FlagThe paper conditions effect estimates on covariates without any statement or check of overlap / common support.

HTE-IDENT-04severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: ivsilent: flag

An IV design should state its exclusion restriction, monotonicity, and first-stage-strength conditions.

In plain wordsA paper using an instrumental variable should state its exclusion restriction (the instrument affects the outcome only through treatment) and monotonicity (no unit is pushed against the instrument's direction), and address how strongly the instrument moves treatment (first-stage strength).
Why it mattersThese are the core conditions under which the instrument gives a valid effect estimate, and a weak instrument undermines it.
CheckWhether the paper states the IV validity conditions (exclusion restriction, monotonicity/no-defiers, relevance / a non-weak first stage).
PassThe paper names the exclusion restriction and monotonicity (or no-defiers) as maintained IV assumptions and speaks to first-stage strength.
FlagThe paper identifies effects via an instrument but states none of the exclusion restriction, monotonicity, or first-stage strength.

HTE-IDENT-05severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: ivsilent: flag

A paper reporting IV estimates should interpret them as complier-local, or justify a broader reading with a homogeneity assumption.

In plain wordsA paper with instrumental-variable estimates should describe them as effects for compliers (units whose treatment follows the instrument), or state that effects are assumed equal across units if it reads them more broadly.
Why it mattersAn instrument reveals the effect only for compliers, so applying the estimate to a wider population needs that extra assumption.
CheckWhether the paper interprets its instrumented estimate as a complier-local effect, or states the homogeneity assumption needed to generalize it beyond compliers.
PassThe paper labels its IV estimate a local / complier effect, or states an effect-homogeneity assumption when reading it as a broader-population effect.
FlagThe paper reports an IV estimate as a population or conditional-average effect with no complier-local caveat and no homogeneity assumption stated.

HTE-IDENT-06severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: generalizationsilent: flag

Generalization to a non-sampled target must state sample-selection unconfoundedness and the effect-modifying covariates adjusted for.

In plain wordsWhen applying its result to a population it did not sample, the paper should state its assumption that, after its adjustments, who was sampled is unrelated to how units respond to treatment, and name the adjusted covariates that change the effect and differ between sample and target.
Why it mattersThe transfer is valid only if the adjustment covers every variable that both changes the effect and differs between sample and target.
CheckWhether the paper states the sample-selection assumptions and names the effect-modifying covariates on which it reweights or stratifies to the target.
PassThe paper states its transport rests on unconfounded sample selection and identifies the effect-modifying covariates (that differ between sample and target) used to reweight or stratify.
FlagThe paper generalizes to a non-sampled target without stating the sample-selection assumption or the effect-modifying covariates it adjusts for.
Further reading
  • Tipton et al. (2012). "Improving Generalizations From Experiments Using Propensity Score Subclassification." https://doi.org/10.3102/1076998612441947, pp. 243, 245

Estimation2

Whether the estimator recovers the target parameter.

HTE-EST-01severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: interaction_modelsilent: flag

An interaction model should include all constitutive terms of the product.

In plain wordsA model with an interaction (a product of two variables) should also include each of those variables on its own.
Why it mattersDropping one of these lower-order terms biases the estimates, and reasons such as an assumed zero average effect, correlation between terms, or centering do not justify leaving it out.
CheckWhether the reported interaction model includes each interacted variable as its own lower-order term alongside the product term.
PassThe specification includes every constitutive term of the interaction together with the product term.
FlagThe model reports a product term while omitting one or more of its constitutive terms.
Further reading
  • Brambor et al. (2006). "Understanding Interaction Models: Improving Empirical Analyses." https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpi014, pp. 66-67
  • Mize et al. (2019). "Best Practices for Estimating, Interpreting, and Presenting Nonlinear Interaction Effects." https://doi.org/10.15195/v6.a4, p. 88
HTE-EST-02severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: nonlinear_interactionsilent: flag

A nonlinear-model interaction effect should be computed from the cross-partial of the predicted outcome, not the product-term coefficient.

In plain wordsIn a nonlinear model, the paper should measure an interaction by how the predicted outcome responds to both variables changing together (the cross-partial), not by the coefficient on the product term.
Why it mattersIn such models the product-term coefficient can be zero when the interaction is not, can have the opposite sign, and the interaction varies across observations.
CheckHow the paper obtains the interaction effect's magnitude: from the estimated cross-partial derivative/difference of predicted outcomes, or by reading the product-term coefficient.
PassThe paper computes the interaction effect as a cross-partial derivative or discrete cross-difference of the predicted outcome (or as marginal effects on the predicted scale), at stated covariate values or averaged over units.
FlagThe paper reports the product-term coefficient (its size or sign) as the interaction effect in a nonlinear model, or reads the effect off a single tool-generated marginal effect for the interacted variable.
Further reading
  • Ai et al. (2003). "Interaction terms in logit and probit models." https://doi.org/10.1016/S0165-1765(03)00032-6, pp. 124, 129
  • Norton et al. (2004). "Computing Interaction Effects and Standard Errors in Logit and Probit Models." https://doi.org/10.1177/1536867X0400400206, p. 3 (PDF pagination)

Inference8

Standard errors, tests, and their validity.

HTE-INFER-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: nonlinear_interactionsilent: flag

Significance of a nonlinear interaction effect should come from the cross-partial's standard error, not the coefficient's test statistic.

In plain wordsIn a nonlinear model, the paper should judge whether an interaction is statistically significant from the standard error of the cross-partial (the interaction's effect on predicted outcomes), not from the test on the product-term coefficient.
Why it mattersThat coefficient's test does not test the interaction effect, which varies with covariates and so is assessed at chosen covariate values or as an average.
CheckHow the paper tests the significance of the interaction effect: from the cross-partial's standard error, or from the product-term coefficient's test statistic.
PassThe paper reports uncertainty / significance for the interaction effect derived from the cross-partial (delta method, simulation, or bootstrap), at specified covariate values or as an average.
FlagThe paper judges interaction significance in a nonlinear model from the z/t-test on the product-term coefficient alone.
Further reading

HTE-INFER-02severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: interaction_modelsilent: flag

Interaction conclusions should rest on conditional marginal effects, not the significance of the product-term coefficient, and constitutive coefficients should not be read as main effects.

In plain wordsThe paper should base conclusions about an interaction on how one variable's effect changes across values of the other (conditional marginal effects), and should not read the coefficient on a single interacted variable as its overall effect.
Why it mattersWhether the product-term coefficient is significant neither proves nor rules out that an effect depends on another variable.
CheckWhether the paper's interaction conclusions turn on conditional marginal effects, or instead on the significance of the product-term coefficient (or on reading a constitutive coefficient as a main effect).
PassThe paper bases its interaction conclusions on the conditional marginal effect(s) across the modifier and does not equate a constitutive coefficient with an unconditional effect.
FlagThe paper decides interaction presence/absence, or keeps/drops the product term, from the product-term coefficient's significance, or interprets a constitutive coefficient as a main/average effect.
Further reading
  • Berry et al. (2012). "Improving Tests of Theories Positing Interaction." https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022381612000199, p. 656

HTE-INFER-03severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: multiple_hypothesessilent: flag

A paper testing a family of hypotheses/outcomes should report multiplicity-adjusted inference or a summary index.

In plain wordsWhen the paper tests a family of related hypotheses or outcomes, it should adjust for the number of tests (for example with Bonferroni, Holm, or false discovery rate methods) or combine them into a summary index.
Why it mattersRunning many tests makes at least one false positive likely, so highlighting only the significant results can mislead.
CheckWhether the paper adjusts inference for the number of tests in the family, or reports a summary index, versus reporting only per-test unadjusted significance.
PassFor the tested family the paper reports a named multiplicity adjustment (Bonferroni, Holm, FWER resampling, Benjamini-Hochberg/FDR) or a summary index, or presents adjusted results alongside unadjusted ones.
FlagThe paper reports only per-test unadjusted p-values / significance for a family of hypotheses or outcomes and highlights the significant subset with no multiplicity adjustment.
PrevalenceAnderson (2008) reports most surveyed randomized evaluations tested five or more outcomes, so unadjusted multiple testing is widespread.

HTE-INFER-04severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: heterogeneity_testsilent: flag

A test for effect variation must isolate variation from the mean shift and validly handle the unknown-ATE nuisance.

In plain wordsA test for whether the treatment effect varies across units should separate that variation from a shift in the average effect, and should properly account for the average effect being unknown, for example with a randomization test over a confidence interval for it.
Why it mattersPlugging an estimated average into a standard test, as in a plain or shifted Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, gives invalid p-values.
CheckWhether the paper's variation test aligns for the average effect and handles the unknown ATE nuisance validly (e.g., a randomization test maximized over an ATE confidence interval), versus a naive distributional test compared to an unadjusted null.
PassThe paper's heterogeneity test aligns for the mean effect and uses a null that accounts for the estimated-ATE nuisance (e.g., FRT over an ATE confidence interval).
FlagThe paper tests for heterogeneity with a naive distributional statistic (e.g., a plain or plug-in shifted Kolmogorov-Smirnov test) compared to the ordinary null distribution.
Further reading
  • Ding et al. (2015). "Randomization Inference for Treatment Effect Variation." https://doi.org/10.1111/rssb.12124, pp. 656, 659
HTE-INFER-05severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: interaction_modelsilent: flag

Marginal-effect significance scanned across a moderator should be corrected for the implied multiple comparisons.

In plain wordsWhen the paper checks at many values of a moderating variable whether the effect is significant, it should correct for these multiple comparisons, for example with a joint test or a bootstrap-based critical value.
Why it mattersStandard intervals at each value are not built for this scan, so reporting any value where the effect looks significant produces too many false positives.
CheckWhether the paper corrects for the many implied comparisons when reporting marginal-effect significance across a moderator, or reports whichever value of the moderator yielded significance using pointwise intervals.
PassThe paper reports marginal-effect significance across the moderator using a multiplicity- or joint-test-corrected procedure (FWER/FDR control or a bootstrap critical value), or tests pre-specified predictions jointly.
FlagThe paper reports a significant conditional marginal effect found by scanning values of the moderator with uncorrected pointwise intervals.
Further reading
  • Esarey et al. (2017). "Marginal Effects in Interaction Models: Determining and Controlling the False Positive Rate." https://doi.org/10.1177/0010414017730080, pp. 1146, 1152
HTE-INFER-06severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: causal_forestsilent: flag

Forest-based confidence intervals must rest on honest, subsampled trees.

In plain wordsWhen the paper reports confidence intervals or standard errors from a causal or random forest, it should state that the trees are honest (each observation either places splits or estimates effects, not both) and grown on subsamples.
Why it mattersWithout honesty and subsampling, the forest's intervals do not cover the true effect.
CheckWhether the paper states the forest was built with honesty and subsampling when it reports forest confidence intervals or standard errors.
PassThe paper states its forest uses honest trees (split-versus-estimate sample separation) and subsampling of the training data for the intervals it reports.
FlagThe paper reports forest confidence intervals or standard errors while stating (or leaving standard) an adaptive/bootstrap forest without honesty.
Further reading
  • Wager et al. (2018). "Estimation and Inference of Heterogeneous Treatment Effects using Random Forests." https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2017.1319839, p. 1237
HTE-INFER-07severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: quantile_distributionalsilent: flag

Quantile/distributional effects should be interpreted as distributional, not individual, absent a rank-preservation assumption.

In plain wordsThe paper should read quantile or distributional effects as differences between the treated and untreated outcome distributions, not as gains or losses for particular individuals, unless it states a rank-preservation assumption (units keep their position in the distribution under treatment).
Why it mattersUnits can change positions between the two groups, so these effects alone do not reveal who gains and who loses.
CheckWhether the paper interprets quantile/distributional effects as statements about the marginal distributions, or reads them as individual-level gains/losses without a rank-preservation (or similar) assumption.
PassThe paper interprets quantile/distributional effects as differences between marginal distributions, and any individual-level or winners/losers claim is qualified by a stated rank-preservation (or equivalent) assumption.
FlagThe paper reads quantile/distributional effects as the effect on particular individuals, or identifies winners and losers, without stating the extra assumption that requires.
Further reading
  • Bitler et al. (2006). "What Mean Impacts Miss: Distributional Effects of Welfare Reform Experiments." https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.96.4.988, pp. 999, 1003-1004
HTE-INFER-08severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: clusteredsilent: flag

A paper with clustered/dependent data should adjust standard errors for that structure.

In plain wordsWhen observations are clustered or otherwise dependent, the paper should adjust its standard errors for that structure, for example with cluster-robust errors or, when clusters are few, a block bootstrap.
Why it mattersStandard errors that treat dependent observations as independent are too small, which overstates confidence in the result.
CheckWhether the paper adjusts its standard errors for the clustered/dependent structure it describes.
PassThe paper reports standard errors that account for the clustering/dependence (cluster-robust errors, or a block bootstrap when clusters are few).
FlagThe paper describes clustered/dependent data but reports standard errors that treat observations as independent.
Further reading
  • Anderson et al. (2008). "Multiple Inference and Gender Differences in the Effects of Early Intervention: A Reevaluation of the Abecedarian, Perry Preschool, and Early Training Projects." https://doi.org/10.1198/016214508000000841, p. 1495

Robustness5

Sensitivity, diagnostics, and alternative specifications.

HTE-ROBUST-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 6 sourcesapplies to: subgroup_analysis, data_driven_htesilent: flag

Heterogeneity claims must be pre-specified or protected by a discovery/inference data split.

In plain wordsThe paper should either specify its subgroups or sources of effect variation before analysis, or find them in one part of the data and test them in another, for example by sample splitting or a held-out test set.
Why it mattersSubgroups chosen after seeing the outcomes can produce spurious findings.
CheckWhether the reported heterogeneity was pre-specified or protected by a discovery-versus-inference data split.
PassThe paper states the subgroups / heterogeneity variables were specified before analysis, OR that discovery and final inference use separate data (sample splitting, a held-out test set, or cross-validation for model/tuning selection).
FlagThe paper reports subgroup or data-driven heterogeneity formed after examining outcomes with no pre-specification and no discovery/inference split.

HTE-ROBUST-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: linear_interactionsilent: flag

A linear interaction with a continuous moderator should have its constant-marginal-effect assumption checked.

In plain wordsWhen the paper lets an effect vary with a continuous variable through a linear interaction, it should check the assumption that the effect changes at a constant rate, for example with a diagnostic plot or a more flexible estimate.
Why it mattersIf the effect does not change at a constant rate, the estimated effects at particular values are biased.
CheckWhether the paper assesses the constant-marginal-effect (linearity) assumption of its linear interaction, e.g. through a diagnostic plot or a binning/flexible estimator, rather than assuming it.
PassThe paper reports a check of the linear-interaction assumption (diagnostic plot, or a binning/kernel/flexible estimate compared against the linear model) and interprets effects consistent with the result.
FlagThe paper reports conditional effects at moderator values from a linear interaction with no assessment of the constant-marginal-effect assumption.
Further reading
  • Hainmueller et al. (2018). "How Much Should We Trust Estimates from Multiplicative Interaction Models? Simple Tools to Improve Empirical Practice." https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2018.46, pp. 167, 172
HTE-ROBUST-03severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: subgroup_heterogeneitysilent: flag

Conditional effects should be reported only where the data are adequate, and sparse-region estimates marked as extrapolation.

In plain wordsThe paper should report effects for subgroups or values of a moderating variable only where it has enough data, or mark estimates in thin regions as extrapolation, for example by showing a histogram or observation counts.
Why it mattersIn regions with few observations, an estimate rests on model assumptions rather than data, so it is fragile.
CheckWhether the paper reports conditional/subgroup effects only where data are adequate, or extends them into sparse regions without flagging the reliance on extrapolation.
PassThe paper limits reported conditional effects to regions with adequate data density (or marks sparse-region estimates as extrapolation, e.g., via a moderator histogram or observation counts).
FlagThe paper reports conditional/subgroup effects in sparse regions of the moderator/covariate space without noting the reliance on extrapolation or the thinness of the data.

  • Grimmer et al. (2017). "Estimating Heterogeneous Treatment Effects and the Effects of Heterogeneous Treatments with Ensemble Methods." https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2017.15, p. 416
HTE-ROBUST-04severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: risk_modelingsilent: flag

A stratifying risk model should be fit blinded to treatment and (if internal) on the whole trial population.

In plain wordsWhen the paper groups units by a model of their baseline risk, it should fit that model without a treatment term and, if built from the study's own data, on all participants rather than the control group alone.
Why it mattersA risk model built otherwise can create or exaggerate apparent differences in treatment effects across risk levels.
CheckWhether the paper states its risk/prognostic model was fit without a treatment-assignment term and, if internal, on the full trial population rather than controls only.
PassThe paper states the risk model was developed blinded to treatment (no treatment-assignment term) and, if internally developed, on the entire trial population.
FlagThe paper stratifies by a risk model without stating it was blinded to treatment, or reports an internal model fit on the control arm only.
Further reading
  • Kent et al. (2019). "The Predictive Approaches to Treatment effect Heterogeneity (PATH) Statement." https://doi.org/10.7326/m18-3667, pp. 40, 48
HTE-ROBUST-05severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: trimmingsilent: pass

A trimming/overlap-selection rule should be based on covariates/propensity, not on outcomes.

In plain wordsWhen the paper trims or restricts its sample to handle poor overlap, it should define the rule from covariates and treatment, such as limits on the propensity score (the estimated chance of treatment), not from outcomes.
Why it mattersA selection rule that uses outcomes introduces bias into the estimated effects.
CheckWhether the paper's trimming / subsample-selection rule is defined from covariates/propensity only, or in a way that uses outcome data.
PassThe paper defines its trimming/overlap-selection rule from covariates and treatment (e.g., propensity-score bounds) without reference to outcomes.
FlagThe paper defines its trimming/selection rule using the outcome distribution (e.g., dropping units by their outcomes or outcome variances).
Further reading
  • Crump et al. (2009). "Dealing with limited overlap in estimation of average treatment effects." https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/asn055, pp. 188, 190

Reporting2

What the paper must state for a reader to evaluate it.

HTE-REPORT-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: nonlinear_interactionsilent: flag

A nonlinear interaction should be reported as predicted values / an effect distribution, not one summary number.

In plain wordsIn a nonlinear model, the paper should present an interaction through predicted outcomes or the spread of the interaction effect across observations, plotted when a key variable is continuous, rather than through one summary number.
Why it mattersThe interaction effect in such models varies in size and direction across observations, so one number cannot describe it.
CheckWhether the paper reports predicted values/probabilities or the distribution of the interaction effect across observations, rather than only one summary number.
PassThe paper reports predicted outcomes/probabilities for the interacting variables, or the distribution of the interaction effect across covariate values (a plot where a focal variable is continuous).
FlagThe paper conveys a nonlinear interaction only through a single summary number or the product-term coefficient, with no predicted values/distribution reported.
Further reading
  • Ai et al. (2003). "Interaction terms in logit and probit models." https://doi.org/10.1016/S0165-1765(03)00032-6, p. 129
  • Mize et al. (2019). "Best Practices for Estimating, Interpreting, and Presenting Nonlinear Interaction Effects." https://doi.org/10.15195/v6.a4, p. 114
HTE-REPORT-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: subgroup_heterogeneitysilent: flag

A paper asserting or denying heterogeneity should state the outcome scale it is measured on.

In plain wordsWhen the paper claims or denies that effects vary, it should state the outcome scale it uses, such as absolute differences, ratios, or a named transformation.
Why it mattersWhether an effect looks constant depends on the scale; a constant relative effect generally means the absolute effect varies.
CheckWhether the paper states the outcome scale (absolute vs. relative, or a named transformation) on which it assesses effect heterogeneity/constancy.
PassThe paper states the scale on which heterogeneity is measured (e.g., absolute difference, ratio, or a named transformation), or reports the effect on more than one scale.
FlagThe paper claims or denies heterogeneity without stating the outcome scale it is measured on.
Further reading
  • Kent et al. (2019). "The Predictive Approaches to Treatment effect Heterogeneity (PATH) Statement." https://doi.org/10.7326/m18-3667, pp. 35-36
  • Ding et al. (2015). "Randomization Inference for Treatment Effect Variation." https://doi.org/10.1111/rssb.12124, p. 658

Data1

Sample construction and measurement.

HTE-DATA-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: attritionsilent: flag

A paper with non-trivial attrition should assess its sensitivity rather than silently analyze complete cases.

In plain wordsWhen a meaningful share of outcomes is missing, the paper should check how sensitive its results are, for example with imputation, worst-case bounds, or re-estimation, or show that missingness is unrelated to treatment, rather than silently analyzing only complete cases.
Why it mattersIf missing outcomes differ by treatment status, comparisons between treated and control groups can be biased.
CheckWhether the paper, given non-trivial attrition/missing outcomes, assesses the sensitivity of its results (imputation, bounds, or re-estimation) rather than reporting complete cases only.
PassThe paper reports an assessment of attrition/missing-outcome sensitivity (imputation, worst-case bounds, or a re-estimation) or evidence attrition is unrelated to treatment.
FlagThe paper reports non-trivial attrition/missing outcomes but analyzes complete cases with no sensitivity assessment.
Further reading
  • Anderson et al. (2008). "Multiple Inference and Gender Differences in the Effects of Early Intervention: A Reevaluation of the Abecedarian, Perry Preschool, and Early Training Projects." https://doi.org/10.1198/016214508000000841, p. 1494
  • Ding et al. (2015). "Randomization Inference for Treatment Effect Variation." https://doi.org/10.1111/rssb.12124, p. 668
