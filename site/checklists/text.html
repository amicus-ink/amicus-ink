The checklists

Text as data

17 checks, each resting on claims from the methods literature. The 9 sources listed at the end are the ones cited as further reading. Each check says when it applies, what passes, what is flagged, and where to read more.

How to read a verdict

Pass and flag are decided by the stated conditions. A check that is out of scope for the paper is marked does not apply; that is never a verdict on what the paper said. When a check applies and the paper says nothing, the verdict is the one given under silent.

A verdict covers only the text the audit could see. A flag on material the paper says is in an appendix or replication package means "not shown here", not "not done", and such flags carry that caveat in the report. Thresholds are rules of thumb unless marked otherwise, and near-threshold values are reported as borderline rather than rounded to a verdict.

When a check applies

A check applies only when its scope trigger fires. Triggers key on the paper's own explicit stance and default to out of scope when the paper is silent.

unsupervised_topic
Fires only when the paper states it fits a topic model or clustering method it names (e.g., LDA, STM, CTM, K-means) to discover themes/categories from text rather than assigning predetermined ones. Does NOT fire for applying predetermined categories with a supervised classifier, or for a dictionary. Ambiguous cases are NA.
topic_covariate_effect
Fires only when the paper states it estimates how an observed covariate or an experimental treatment relates to topic prevalence or topic content (e.g., regresses topic proportions on metadata, or an STM prevalence covariate). Does NOT fire when the paper fits a topic model and only labels/reads topics without relating them to a covariate. Ambiguous cases are NA.
topic_covariate_effects
Fires when the paper states it estimates a relationship between document metadata/covariates and estimated topic proportions (a covariate-topic effect it reports with inference). Does NOT fire when a topic model is reported only descriptively with no covariate-topic relationship, nor when covariates are used only to label documents.
topic_treatment_effect
Fires when the paper states that an experimentally assigned or otherwise treatment variable is the covariate whose effect on estimated topics it reports. Does NOT fire for an observational (non-assigned) covariate, nor for a treatment whose effect is estimated on a non-text outcome.
supervised_classification
Fires only when the paper states in its own words that it trains a supervised learning model on labeled/hand-coded documents to assign documents to predetermined categories. Does NOT fire for: applying a pre-built dictionary to score documents (that is `dictionary`); fitting a topic model or clustering to discover categories (that is `unsupervised_topic`); a coder never infers 'supervised' from the mere presence of categories in the paper. Undeclared or ambiguous cases are out of scope (NA).
cross_validated_performance
Fires only when the paper states it estimates a classifier's accuracy using cross-validation or an explicit train/test split. Does NOT fire when the paper reports only in-sample fit, or reports accuracy computed on the full labeled set with no held-out partition. Ambiguous cases are NA.
dictionary
Fires only when the paper states it measures a quantity by applying a word list, dictionary, or lexicon (counting or scoring words against a fixed list). Does NOT fire for a supervised classifier trained on labeled text, or for embeddings. Ambiguous cases are NA.
scaling
Fires only when the paper states it places texts or actors on a latent dimension using a text-scaling method it names (e.g., wordscores, wordfish). Does NOT fire for estimating topic proportions, or for a supervised classifier. Ambiguous cases are NA.
embeddings
Fires only when the paper states it uses word or document embeddings it names (e.g., word2vec, GloVe) as a measure of meaning or similarity. Does NOT fire for a bag-of-words topic model, or when embeddings are used purely as black-box input features to a supervised task whose outputs are validated separately. Ambiguous cases are NA.
word_embeddings
Fires when the paper states it uses word embeddings to measure meaning or similarity, it names word2vec, GloVe, fastText, or 'word embeddings'. Does NOT fire for a bag-of-words topic model, nor for using a large language model to classify text without embedding vectors.
llm_synthetic
Fires only when the paper states it uses a large language model to generate synthetic survey, opinion, or respondent data that it then analyzes as if it were data. Does NOT fire when an LLM is used to classify or annotate existing human-authored texts, or only as a writing aid. Ambiguous cases are NA.
multilingual
Fires only when the paper states its source corpus contains documents in more than one language and analyzes them jointly (whether or not they are first translated into a common language). Does NOT fire for a single-language corpus, even a non-English one. Ambiguous cases are NA.
all
Every paper that uses an automated or quantitative text-analysis method (dictionary, supervised classifier, topic model/clustering, scaling, embeddings, or LLM generation) to produce a measure or quantity from a body of text.

Estimation1

Whether the estimator recovers the target parameter.

TEXT-EST-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: unsupervised_topicsilent: flag

A paper using an unsupervised topic/cluster model must justify its topic count and model choice on substantive grounds, not statistical fit alone.

In plain wordsA paper using a topic or clustering model, which sorts texts into groups on its own, should justify its number of topics and its chosen model on substantive grounds, such as interpretability or checks against labels, not a statistical fit score alone.
Why it mattersA fit score alone does not show that the topics capture the meaningful content the paper's conclusions depend on.
CheckThe basis the paper gives for its number of topics/clusters and its choice among candidate models.
PassThe paper reports selecting the topic/cluster count and model on a substantive basis, interpretability, reading of exemplars, joint coherence-and-exclusivity, covariate/label validation, or a pre-registered word-set criterion.
FlagThe paper selects the number of topics or the model solely by a statistical fit statistic (held-out likelihood, perplexity, or marginal likelihood) with no substantive check, or states no basis at all for the count it used.
DisputedGrün (2011) recommends choosing the topic count in a data-driven way, using held-out perplexity, which treats statistical fit alone as sufficient.

Inference3

Standard errors, tests, and their validity.

TEXT-INFER-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: topic_covariate_effectsilent: flag

A paper estimating covariate effects on topic proportions must propagate topic-estimation uncertainty into its reported intervals.

In plain wordsWhen the paper relates topic shares to other variables, its confidence intervals should include the uncertainty from estimating those topic shares, rather than treating the shares as known.
Why it mattersTopic shares are estimated, not observed, so ignoring that step makes the results look more certain than they are.
CheckWhether the paper, when it reports intervals/standard errors for topic-covariate relationships, states or uses a mechanism that carries the uncertainty of the estimated topic proportions into those intervals, rather than plugging in point estimates as if the proportions were known.
PassThe paper states it propagates topic-estimation uncertainty into its downstream intervals (e.g., a method-of-composition / uncertainty-propagating estimator, or the default uncertainty-carrying setting of its estimator).
FlagThe paper reports intervals or tests for topic-covariate effects and neither states nor uses any mechanism carrying topic-estimation uncertainty, i.e., it treats the estimated topic proportions as fixed and known.
Further reading
  • Margaret E. Roberts, Brandon Stewart, & Dustin Tingley. (2019). <b>stm</b>: An <i>R</i> Package for Structural Topic Models. Journal of Statistical Software, 91(2). https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v091.i02, p. 15 (PDF pagination)
TEXT-INFER-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: topic_covariate_effectssilent: flag

A paper testing effects across many topics must address multiple testing via FDR control or a pre-analysis plan.

In plain wordsWhen the paper tests effects across many topics, it should guard against chance findings from running many tests, for example by controlling the false discovery rate (the expected share of false positives) or following a pre-analysis plan.
Why it mattersMany tests do not by themselves give accurate p-values, so significant topics picked out without a safeguard are unreliable.
CheckWhether the paper, when it reports significance of effects across many topics, applies a multiple-testing correction or a pre-analysis plan that limits the number of hypotheses tested.
PassThe paper reports a multiple-testing safeguard (FDR control, a pre-analysis plan, or an equivalent) for its topic-level effect tests, or does not select significant effects from a large family of topic-level tests.
FlagThe paper reports significant covariate/treatment effects selected from many topic-level tests with no correction for multiplicity.

TEXT-INFER-03severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: topic_treatment_effectsilent: flag

When a treatment is used to both estimate topics and estimate its effect, the paper must report a safeguard against a spurious, baked-in effect.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses the treatment both to build its topics and to estimate the treatment's effect on them, it should report a safeguard, such as a permutation test that shuffles treatment labels and refits, showing the effect is not spurious.
Why it mattersUsing the treatment in both steps can build the conclusion into the measure itself and produce false positive findings.
CheckWhether the paper, when the treatment variable is used both in fitting the topics and in estimating the effect, reports a check that the estimated effect is not spurious, e.g., a permutation test that reshuffles the treatment label and refits, finding no spurious effect.
PassThe paper reports a permutation test or an equivalent safeguard against a baked-in/false-positive effect when the treatment is used for both measurement and effect estimation.
FlagThe paper uses the treatment for both topic estimation and effect estimation and reports the effect with no safeguard against circularity.

Robustness12

Sensitivity, diagnostics, and alternative specifications.

TEXT-ROBUST-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: supervised_classificationsilent: flag

A paper using a supervised classifier must demonstrate, on held-out documents, that its automated labels reproduce human coding.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses a supervised classifier, a model trained on hand-coded examples to label texts, it should report how well its labels match human coding on documents not used in training.
Why it mattersThe classifier's labels can be trusted only once the paper shows it reproduces human coding on documents it was not trained on.
CheckWhether the paper reports the agreement between the classifier's labels and human/hand-coded labels, computed on documents held out of training (an out-of-sample or cross-validated comparison).
PassThe paper reports an out-of-sample agreement between machine labels and human coding (e.g., held-out accuracy, precision/recall, or agreement on a test set not used to fit the model).
FlagThe paper reports no comparison to human coding, or reports agreement computed only on the training documents (in-sample) with no held-out check. If only in-sample agreement is given, treat as a FLAG, the out-of-sample demonstration is the requirement, and a held-out figure in an appendix or supplement counts as satisfying it.
Further reading
  • Justin Grimmer & Brandon Stewart. (2013). Text as Data: The Promise and Pitfalls of Automatic Content Analysis Methods for Political Texts. Political Analysis, 21(3), 267-297. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mps028, pp. 269, 271, 279
TEXT-ROBUST-02severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: cross_validated_performancesilent: flag

A paper reporting cross-validated classifier performance must perform predictor selection and tuning within the folds, not on the full labeled data.

In plain wordsIf the paper reports cross-validated classifier accuracy, it should state that choosing predictors and tuning the model happened only within each training split, not on all labeled data beforehand.
Why it mattersCross-validated accuracy is a valid estimate of performance only when every modeling step stays inside the training data of each split.
CheckWhether a paper that reports cross-validated (or train/test) classifier accuracy states that feature/predictor selection and tuning were done within each training fold, not on all labeled documents before splitting.
PassThe paper states that all modeling steps that touch the outcome, feature/predictor selection, tuning, were performed inside the training portion of each cross-validation fold.
FlagThe paper reports cross-validated accuracy but selects predictors or tunes on the full labeled set before cross-validating, or does not indicate where selection occurred so leakage cannot be ruled out.
Further reading

TEXT-ROBUST-03severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: supervised_classificationsilent: flag

A paper using a supervised classifier must show its training documents are a random or representative sample of the corpus to be coded.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses a supervised classifier, it should show that its hand-coded training documents are a random or otherwise representative sample of the texts the classifier will label.
Why it mattersClassifiers assume the training documents are a random sample of the full collection, so the paper needs to show this holds.
CheckHow the paper describes the selection of its training/hand-coded documents, and whether that selection is a random or otherwise representative sample of the corpus the classifier will label.
PassThe paper states its training documents were randomly sampled from, or otherwise shown to represent, the population of documents to be coded.
FlagThe paper draws training documents by a non-representative route (keyword hits, convenience, one time period) or does not describe how training documents were selected.

TEXT-ROBUST-04severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: dictionarysilent: flag

A paper using a dictionary must establish that its word list fits the target domain (or build a domain-specific one) and validate the resulting measures.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses a dictionary, a word list that scores texts, it should show the list fits the texts it studies, for example by checking scored documents against hand coding, or build a list for those texts.
Why it mattersA dictionary gives valid measures only when its word scores match how the words are actually used in the paper's texts.
CheckWhether the paper presents evidence that its dictionary measures the intended quantity in the target corpus: validation against hand-coding/exemplars, or that the list was constructed or shown to fit this domain.
PassThe paper reports validating its dictionary measures against the target corpus (e.g., checking scored documents against hand reading/coding), OR states it built a problem-specific dictionary for this corpus.
FlagThe paper applies an off-the-shelf dictionary developed for another domain and treats its output as correct with no evidence that the word scores fit the target texts.

TEXT-ROBUST-05severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: scalingsilent: flag

A paper using a text-scaling method must validate that the recovered dimension is the concept it claims to measure.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses a text scaling method, which places texts along a single dimension, it should show that the dimension is the concept it claims, such as by comparing to expert placements or reading texts at the extremes.
Why it mattersScaling picks up whatever variation dominates the language, which may be style or other content instead of the intended concept, such as ideology.
CheckWhether the paper presents evidence that the recovered scale corresponds to the concept it is claimed to measure, comparison to external placements/expert scores, or reading of texts at the extremes.
PassThe paper reports validating the recovered dimension against an external benchmark or substantive reading that confirms it is the intended concept.
FlagThe paper interprets the scaling output as the intended dimension (e.g., ideology) with no validation that the recovered space is that dimension and not another dominant source of variation.

TEXT-ROBUST-06severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 8 sourcesapplies to: unsupervised_topicsilent: flag

A paper using unsupervised topics/clusters as measures must validate that they correspond to the concepts claimed, via reading exemplars, external comparison, or combined substantive evidence.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses topics or clusters as measures, it should show they capture the concepts claimed, for example by reading the most typical documents, comparing to outside labels or human coding, or combining other validity evidence.
Why it mattersModels that find groups on their own are not guaranteed to produce meaningful categories, so the paper needs evidence beyond top-word lists.
CheckWhether the paper reports at least one activity establishing that its topics/clusters measure what it says they do, drawn from: (a) reading the documents most associated with each topic/cluster; (b) checking topic word lists against exemplar documents; (c) comparing topics to external labels, human coding, or metadata; (d) assembling experimental, substantive, and statistical evidence of conceptual validity.
PassThe paper reports at least one of the enumerated validation activities for the topics/clusters it uses as measures.
FlagThe paper uses topics/clusters as measures while reporting nothing beyond the top-word lists the model produces, no reading of documents, no external comparison, no other validity evidence.

TEXT-ROBUST-07severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 6 sourcesapplies to: unsupervised_topic, embeddingssilent: flag

A paper fitting a topic model or embeddings must address multimodality/initialization sensitivity through deterministic initialization, multiple starts, or averaging over runs.

In plain wordsIf the paper fits a topic model or embeddings (numeric representations of word or document meaning), it should address how results can change with the random starting point, such as by using a fixed starting method, fitting from several starts, or averaging over runs.
Why it mattersThese models can settle on different solutions depending on where they start, so results from a single random run may not replicate.
CheckWhether the paper addresses sensitivity of its fitted model to initialization/run-to-run variability, via one of: a deterministic (spectral) initialization; fitting from multiple starts and selecting/diagnosing among runs; or, for embeddings, averaging the quantity of interest over multiple randomly initialized runs (or showing the chosen specification is stable).
PassThe paper reports at least one of the enumerated remedies for estimation instability.
FlagThe paper reports a single fit from a single (random) initialization and does not mention multimodality, initialization sensitivity, or run-to-run stability.

TEXT-ROBUST-08severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: word_embeddingssilent: flag

A paper that trains embeddings locally must account for run-to-run estimation instability rather than rely on a single fit; a paper using pretrained large-corpus embeddings satisfies this.

In plain wordsIf the paper trains word embeddings, numeric representations of word meaning, on its own texts, it should fit them several times and combine or compare the results; using embeddings pretrained on a large outside corpus also satisfies this.
Why it mattersLocally trained embeddings vary from run to run on the same texts, so conclusions from a single run may be mistaken and not replicable.
CheckWhen the paper trains embeddings locally on its own corpus: whether it reports fitting them multiple times from different random initializations and aggregating (e.g., averaging the distance/similarity metric) or otherwise assessing run-to-run stability. When the paper instead relies on pretrained/off-the-shelf embeddings: whether it identifies that choice.
PassThe paper reports averaging/aggregating across several local embedding runs or a stability assessment; OR it relies on pretrained embeddings trained on a large external corpus; OR it otherwise justifies that repeated fits are unnecessary for its specification.
FlagThe paper trains embeddings locally on its own corpus and draws substantive conclusions from a single run with no treatment of estimation instability.
Further reading

TEXT-ROBUST-09severity if flagged: highlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: llm_syntheticsilent: flag

A paper using LLM synthetic responses for inference must validate them against real human data on the conditional relationships it studies, not just on marginal means.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses survey responses generated by a large language model (LLM) for inference, it should compare them with real human data on the relationships it studies, such as subgroup differences, not just on averages.
Why it mattersLLM-generated responses are unreliable for inference and do worse on relationships between variables than on simple averages.
CheckWhether the paper benchmarks its LLM synthetic data against real survey/human data on the conditional relationships (regression coefficients, subgroup differences) it relies on, not only on marginal means.
PassThe paper compares its synthetic data to real human data on the specific conditional/associational relationships it uses, and its inferences rest on that comparison.
FlagThe paper uses LLM synthetic responses for inference while validating them only on aggregate means, or without benchmarking them against real human data at all.

TEXT-ROBUST-10severity if flagged: highlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: multilingualsilent: flag

A paper jointly analyzing a multilingual corpus must validate that its topics/measures are comparable across languages and not translation or style artifacts.

In plain wordsIf the paper analyzes texts in several languages together, it should check that its topics or measures mean the same across languages, for example by confirming no topic belongs to one language or reading original-language documents.
Why it mattersStandard methods assume a single language, so pooled results may reflect translation or writing style instead of shared meaning.
CheckWhether the paper reports a cross-language comparability check on its topics/measures, drawn from: verifying no topic is confined to a single source language; reading native-language documents for a topic to confirm meaning survives; or modeling original language as a content covariate to separate semantics from translation artifacts.
PassThe paper reports at least one of the enumerated cross-language comparability checks for the multilingual measures it uses.
FlagThe paper pools multiple languages (via translation or joint modeling) and interprets the resulting topics/measures with no check that they are comparable across languages rather than translation/language artifacts.

TEXT-ROBUST-11severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: embeddingssilent: flag

A paper using embeddings as a measure of meaning must evaluate their quality for that intended use.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses word or document embeddings to measure meaning or similarity, it should evaluate whether they work for that purpose, for example by comparing them with human judgments or checking nearest-neighbor rankings.
Why it mattersEmbeddings that work well as inputs to other models need not capture meaning, so off-the-shelf quality cannot be assumed.
CheckWhether the paper reports any evaluation of its embeddings' fitness for its intended measurement task, e.g., query/nearest-neighbor ranking correlations, comparison to human judgments, comparison to a model known to perform well, or another task-appropriate check on domain-relevant queries.
PassThe paper reports an evaluation of whether its embeddings are fit for the intended measurement use (any task-appropriate check, e.g., query/nearest-neighbor ranking, a human comparison, or a known-good-model comparison), rather than assuming off-the-shelf quality.
FlagThe paper uses embeddings as a measure of meaning/similarity and reports no evaluation of their quality for that purpose.

A paper using any automated text method must report validation of its measure in its own application.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses any automated text method, it should report at least one check that the resulting measure works in its own application.
Why it mattersEvery automated text method is a simplified model of language, so how well it performs on a new set of texts cannot be assumed.

Reporting1

What the paper must state for a reader to evaluate it.

TEXT-REPORT-01severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: llm_syntheticsilent: flag

A paper using LLM-generated data must report the exact prompts and the specific model version/date used.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses data generated by a large language model (LLM), it should report the exact prompts and the specific model version and date used, in the paper or its replication materials.
Why it mattersLLM output changes with prompt wording and with model updates outside the researcher's control, so these details are needed to reproduce the analysis.
CheckWhether the paper reports the verbatim prompt(s) used to generate the synthetic data and the specific model identifier/version and date it was queried.
PassThe paper reports both the exact prompt text and the model version/date (in the paper or its replication materials).
FlagThe paper omits the exact prompt(s) or the specific model version/date used to generate the synthetic data.
