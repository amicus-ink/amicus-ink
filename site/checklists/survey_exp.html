The checklists

Survey experiments

24 checks, each resting on claims from the methods literature. The 23 sources listed at the end are the ones cited as further reading. Each check says when it applies, what passes, what is flagged, and where to read more.

How to read a verdict

Pass and flag are decided by the stated conditions. A check that is out of scope for the paper is marked does not apply; that is never a verdict on what the paper said. When a check applies and the paper says nothing, the verdict is the one given under silent.

A verdict covers only the text the audit could see. A flag on material the paper says is in an appendix or replication package means "not shown here", not "not done", and such flags carry that caveat in the report. Thresholds are rules of thumb unless marked otherwise, and near-threshold values are reported as borderline rather than rounded to a verdict.

When a check applies

A check applies only when its scope trigger fires. Triggers key on the paper's own explicit stance and default to out of scope when the paper is silent.

all
Every survey experiment in the corpus.
list_experiment
The paper states it uses a list experiment / item-count technique, respondents in a treatment group receive a list containing a sensitive item and report only a count. Fires on the paper's own naming of a list/item-count/unmatched-count design. Does NOT fire on a direct sensitive question, a randomized-response design, or an endorsement design.
nonrandomized_moderator
The paper states it estimates or interprets the effect of a variable it did NOT randomly assign, a measured respondent characteristic, attitude, exposure, or covariate used as an explanatory, moderating, or mediating variable in a causal claim. Fires when the paper reads a non-randomized variable's association as its effect. Does NOT fire on effects of the randomized treatment itself, nor on associations the paper explicitly labels non-causal.
conjoint
The paper states it uses a conjoint / fully-randomized attribute design in which respondents evaluate profiles whose attributes are independently randomized. Fires on the paper's own naming of a conjoint or randomized-attribute profile design. Does NOT fire on a single-vignette design that manipulates one or two features, nor on a list/endorsement design.
factor_isolation_claim
The paper states its manipulation isolates the effect of one specific factor, attributing the treatment-control difference to that single factor. A design presented only as estimating the combined effect of a bundled treatment, with no claim to isolate one factor, does NOT fire it.
endorsement_experiment
The paper states it uses an endorsement experiment, respondents rate policies, and a randomly assigned subset sees the policy endorsed by an actor. Fires on the paper's own naming of an endorsement design. Does NOT fire on a list experiment or a direct actor-approval question.
belief_effect
The paper states its estimand is the effect of a belief or of respondents' understanding of a manipulated attribute (an epistemic effect), it interprets its manipulation as changing what respondents believe about a focal factor. Fires on the paper's own framing of the quantity of interest as a belief/attribute effect. Does NOT fire on a design that interprets its manipulation only as the effect of the message/wording itself, with no belief-level claim.
mediation
The paper states it performs a mediation analysis or decomposes an effect through a mediator that it measured rather than randomized. Fires on the paper's own naming of a mediator / indirect effect with an observed mediator. Does NOT fire on a design that randomizes the mediator in a factorial arm, nor on a paper with no mediation claim.
subgroup_or_conditioning
The paper states it estimates a subgroup, conditional, or moderated effect, or conditions/subsets its estimate on a respondent-measured variable. Fires when the paper reports heterogeneous effects by a respondent variable, interacts treatment with a covariate, or conditions on a post-treatment quantity. Does NOT fire on a paper reporting only a single overall treatment effect with no conditioning.
direct_list_comparison
The paper states it reads the gap between a direct-question estimate and a list-experiment (or other indirect) estimate as a measure of sensitivity or social-desirability bias. A list experiment alone, a direct question alone, or using both only to triangulate a prevalence without interpreting their difference as bias, does NOT fire it.
survey_weights
The paper states it applies survey/selection/sampling weights in estimating a population-level (PATE) effect. Reporting demographics or design features without using weights in estimation does NOT fire it.
list_regression
The paper states it estimates a regression model (linear least squares, nonlinear least squares, or maximum likelihood) relating respondent covariates to list-experiment responses. Fires when the paper names such a multivariate list-experiment estimator. Does NOT fire on a paper reporting only a difference-in-means prevalence or unadjusted subgroup means.
list_predictor
The paper states it uses a list-experiment quantity (a predicted/fitted sensitive-item value or estimated proportion) as an explanatory variable in a further outcome model, a two-step or multi-stage procedure. Fires on the paper's own description of feeding a list-experiment estimate into a downstream regression. Does NOT fire on a one-stage list-experiment analysis that reports prevalence or covariate associations directly.
repeated_tasks
The paper states each respondent contributes more than one observation to the analysis, multiple conjoint tasks, repeated rating/choice tasks, repeated vignettes, or multiple items analyzed jointly per respondent. Fires on the paper's own description of a within-respondent repeated-measures structure. Does NOT fire on a between-subjects design where each respondent contributes a single observation.
population_inference
The paper states it generalizes its treatment-effect estimate to a real-world or target population, it describes its estimand as a population effect, claims representativeness, or applies survey weights to a population. Fires on the paper's own generalization/representativeness language. Does NOT fire on a paper that explicitly confines its claims to the study sample or presents itself as an internal-validity demonstration.
framing_treatment
The paper states it compares two or more substantive framing/wording/message arms to estimate a treatment effect on attitudes. Fires when the design contrasts message or frame conditions. Does NOT fire on list/conjoint/endorsement measurement designs where a control list or reference category is intrinsic to the method.

The assumptions the causal claim rests on.

SURVEY_EXP-IDENT-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A survey experiment must state that assignment to its conditions was random.

In plain wordsThe paper should state that respondents were assigned to experimental conditions (such as arms, lists, or profiles) at random.
Why it mattersRandom assignment makes the groups alike on average, so a difference in outcomes can be read as the effect of the treatment.
CheckWhether the paper states that respondents were assigned to conditions (arms, lists, profiles) at random.
PassThe paper states that assignment to experimental conditions was randomized.
FlagThe paper reports an experimental contrast but never states that assignment was random.

SURVEY_EXP-IDENT-02severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 8 sourcesapplies to: list_experimentsilent: flag

A list-experiment paper must state or address the no-design-effect identifying assumption.

In plain wordsA paper using a list experiment (where respondents report how many items on a list apply to them) should state or address the assumption that adding the sensitive item does not change answers to the other items.
Why it mattersThe estimate of how common the sensitive item is holds only if adding it leaves each respondent's count of the other items unchanged; otherwise the estimate is mismeasured.
CheckWhether the paper states, or otherwise addresses, the assumption that the sensitive item does not shift control-item answers.
PassThe paper states the no-design-effect assumption (that appending the sensitive item leaves control-item responses unchanged) or otherwise addresses it.
FlagThe paper analyzes a list experiment without ever stating or addressing the no-design-effect assumption.

In plain wordsA list-experiment paper should acknowledge that respondents shown the sensitive item must count it truthfully, and should discuss whether truthful reporting is plausible for its item.

A causal claim about a non-randomized covariate or moderator must state the exogeneity / no-unobserved-confounding assumption it rests on.

In plain wordsWhen the paper reads the effect of a variable that was not randomly assigned as causal, it should state the assumption that no unmeasured factor drives both that variable and the outcome, or label the relationship as not causal.
Why it mattersRandom assignment supports causal claims only about the randomized treatment, so causal claims about other variables rest on this further assumption.
CheckWhether, when the paper interprets a non-randomized variable's effect causally, it states the identifying assumption (no unobserved confounding / exogeneity) that would license that reading.
PassThe paper states the identifying assumption (exogeneity / no unobserved confounding) that would license the causal reading of the non-randomized variable, or explicitly labels the relationship as non-causal.
FlagThe paper interprets a non-randomized variable's effect causally without stating any exogeneity / no-confounding assumption.

SURVEY_EXP-IDENT-05severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: conjointsilent: flag

A multi-task conjoint paper must state or address the stability / no-carryover assumption.

In plain wordsA conjoint paper (where respondents choose among profiles with randomly varied attributes) that gives each respondent several tasks should state or address the assumption that answers in one task are unaffected by profiles seen in other tasks.
Why it mattersEstimating attribute effects from several tasks per respondent requires that responses stay stable across tasks and do not carry over from other tasks.
CheckWhether the paper states or addresses the stability / no-carryover assumption across a respondent's tasks.
PassThe paper states or addresses the stability / no-carryover assumption (potential outcomes stable across tasks, unaffected by other tasks' profiles).
FlagThe paper analyzes a multi-task conjoint without stating or addressing stability / no-carryover across tasks.

SURVEY_EXP-IDENT-06severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: conjointsilent: flag

A conjoint paper must confine its causal quantities to attribute combinations the randomization assigns positive probability, or acknowledge the restriction.

In plain wordsA conjoint paper should limit its causal claims to attribute combinations that the randomization could actually produce, or acknowledge where some combinations were excluded.
Why it mattersEffects can be estimated only for combinations that had a chance of being shown; going beyond them requires extra assumptions.
CheckWhether the paper's randomization assigns positive probability to every attribute combination whose effect it analyzes (or the paper acknowledges the restricted support where combinations are excluded).
PassThe paper's attributes are randomized so that every analyzed attribute combination has positive assignment probability, or the paper restricts its causal claims to the combinations actually assigned.
FlagThe paper analyzes conjoint effects over attribute combinations that its randomization excludes or never assigns, without acknowledging the restricted support.

SURVEY_EXP-IDENT-07severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 7 sourcesapplies to: factor_isolation_claimsilent: flag

A contrast can be attributed to one intended factor only if the compared conditions differ solely on that factor (or the effect operates only through the intended channel); a paper that claims to isolate one factor must either state/defend that single-factor (single-channel) isolation or acknowledge that the contrast bundles several differences and limit its attribution accordingly.

In plain wordsWhen the paper credits an effect to one factor, it should defend that the compared conditions differ only on that factor (or that the effect works only through the intended channel), or acknowledge that the conditions differ in several ways and limit its claims.
Why it mattersIf the conditions differ in more than one way, the effect cannot be credited to the single intended factor.
CheckWhether the paper, when it attributes an effect to one intended factor, either states/defends that its conditions differ only on that factor (or that the effect operates only through the intended channel) or acknowledges that the contrast bundles multiple differences.
PassThe paper states or defends that its compared conditions differ only on the named factor (or that the effect operates only through the intended causal channel), or acknowledges that the contrast bundles multiple differences and limits its attribution accordingly.
FlagThe paper attributes the effect to one intended factor without either defending single-factor/single-channel isolation or acknowledging that its conditions differ on additional dimensions it does not account for.

A mediation analysis with an observed mediator must state its sequential-ignorability assumption.

In plain wordsA paper that studies how an effect passes through a measured, not randomized, intermediate variable (a mediator) should state the sequential-ignorability assumption: that nothing beyond the treatment and pretreatment covariates confounds the link between the mediator and the outcome.

Subgroup/conditioning variables must be pretreatment, or shown unaffected by treatment.

In plain wordsVariables used to split the sample or define subgroups should be measured before treatment, or the paper should show the treatment did not affect them.
Why it mattersConditioning on a variable measured after treatment can bias results and change what is being estimated.

FlagThe paper conditions on or subsets by a variable measured after treatment without showing the treatment left it uncontaminated.

SURVEY_EXP-IDENT-13severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: direct_list_comparisonsilent: flag

The gap between a list-experiment estimate and a direct-question estimate can be read as sensitivity/social-desirability bias only if the list assumptions hold and, further, there is no differential nonresponse, no order or wording effects, and a common population; a paper making this interpretation must state those auxiliary assumptions.

In plain wordsA paper reading the gap between list-experiment and direct-question estimates as sensitivity bias should state the assumptions this needs (the list assumptions, no differences in nonresponse, no order or wording effects, and a common population), or note that without them the gap may reflect only the measurement methods.
Why it mattersWithout these assumptions, the gap may reflect only the difference between the two measurement methods.
CheckWhether the paper interpreting the list-minus-direct gap as sensitivity bias states the required auxiliary assumptions (list assumptions plus no differential nonresponse/order/wording, common population).
PassThe paper states the auxiliary assumptions its bias interpretation needs, or notes that without them the gap only reflects the measurement technology.
FlagThe paper reads the list-versus-direct difference as sensitivity bias without stating the auxiliary assumptions it requires.

Estimation2

Whether the estimator recovers the target parameter.

SURVEY_EXP-EST-01severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: list_experimentsilent: pass

A list-experiment paper should not present multivariate covariate models of the sensitive trait, without caveat, when the estimated prevalence is negative or indistinguishable from zero.

In plain wordsA list-experiment paper should not model how the sensitive trait relates to several respondent characteristics, without a caveat, when the estimated share holding the trait is negative or not distinguishable from zero.
Why it mattersA trait must actually be present to vary with respondent characteristics, so such models are unreliable when almost no one holds it.
CheckWhether the paper presents a multivariate covariate model of the sensitive trait while its estimated prevalence is negative or not distinguishable from zero.
PassEither the paper presents no multivariate covariate model of the sensitive trait, or where it does the estimated prevalence is clearly positive (point estimate positive and not statistically indistinguishable from zero).
FlagThe paper presents a multivariate covariate model of the sensitive trait while its estimated prevalence is negative or statistically indistinguishable from zero, with no stated caveat about the trait's rarity limiting model reliability.
Thresholdcutoff 0.0; provenance: rule of thumb; borderline band: [0.0, 0.05]
Inside the bandBelow the cutoff (estimated prevalence negative): FLAG if a multivariate covariate model is presented without a caveat about unreliability. In the border band (prevalence non-negative but statistically indistinguishable from zero, i.e. its confidence interval includes zero): FLAG if a multivariate model is presented without a stated caveat about the trait's rarity limiting model reliability. Above the band (prevalence point estimate positive and its interval excludes zero): PASS.

SURVEY_EXP-EST-02severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: survey_weightssilent: flag

A survey-weighted population (PATE) estimator is derived assuming the weights are correct and known and that treatment assignment is independent of selection, and it can be arbitrarily biased when an unmodeled selection mechanism makes the weights wrong; a paper using weights to estimate a population effect must state these assumptions or acknowledge weight-misspecification bias.

In plain wordsA paper using survey weights to estimate a population-wide effect should state that this assumes correct, known weights and treatment assignment independent of selection into the sample, or acknowledge bias from wrong weights.
Why it mattersIf an unmodeled selection process makes the weights wrong, the weighted estimate can be badly biased.
CheckWhether the paper using survey weights for a population effect states the assumptions its weighted estimator relies on (correct/known weights, assignment independent of selection) or acknowledges weight-misspecification bias.
PassThe paper states the selection/weighting assumptions behind its weighted PATE estimate, or acknowledges the bias possible when the weights are misspecified.
FlagThe paper reports a survey-weighted population effect without stating the weighting/selection assumptions or acknowledging weight-misspecification bias.
Further reading

A linear-regression list-experiment analysis must use heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors.

In plain wordsA list-experiment paper using linear regression should report heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, which allow the spread of errors to differ across groups.
Why it mattersThe error spread differs by treatment status in this regression, so ordinary standard errors are wrong.
CheckWhether the reported standard errors for the list-experiment regression are heteroskedasticity-robust.
PassThe paper reports heteroskedasticity-robust (heteroskedasticity-consistent) standard errors for its linear list-experiment regression.
FlagThe paper reports a linear list-experiment regression with ordinary (non-robust) standard errors.

A two-step list-experiment-as-predictor analysis must propagate first-stage uncertainty into its standard errors.

In plain wordsWhen the paper uses a list-experiment estimate as an input to a second model, its standard errors should include the uncertainty from the first estimate.
Why it mattersPlugging in first-stage estimates as if they were known gets neither the coefficient nor the standard error right.
CheckWhether the paper's standard errors for the downstream model incorporate first-stage (list-experiment) estimation uncertainty, via bootstrap or an analytic multi-stage variance.
PassThe paper's two-stage standard errors incorporate the uncertainty from both the list-experiment stage and the outcome stage.
FlagThe paper feeds a list-experiment estimate into an outcome model but reports standard errors that ignore the first-stage uncertainty.

In plain wordsWhen each respondent gives several answers, the standard errors should account for this, by clustering on respondent or using a respondent-level bootstrap.

FlagThe paper analyzes repeated within-respondent observations with default standard errors that ignore the within-respondent correlation.

Robustness2

Sensitivity, diagnostics, and alternative specifications.

SURVEY_EXP-ROBUST-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 7 sourcesapplies to: list_experimentsilent: flag

A list-experiment paper should report a diagnostic (respondent-type proportions or a design-effect test) bearing on its identifying assumptions.

In plain wordsA list-experiment paper should report a check on its key assumptions, such as estimated shares of respondent types or a formal test for design effects (the sensitive item changing answers to other items).
Why it mattersA negative estimated share of a respondent type signals that an assumption fails, and a formal test can detect a violation.
CheckWhether the paper reports a diagnostic for identification-assumption violations, estimated respondent-type proportions and/or a formal design-effect test.
PassThe paper reports estimated respondent-type proportions and/or a formal design-effect (assumption-violation) test as a diagnostic on its list-experiment identifying assumptions.
FlagThe paper reports a list-experiment estimate but no proportion-based or test-based diagnostic for the identifying assumptions.
Further reading
  • Graeme Blair & Kosuke Imai. (2012). Statistical Analysis of List Experiments. Political Analysis, 20(1), 47-77. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpr048, pp. 64, 72
SURVEY_EXP-ROBUST-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: belief_effectsilent: flag

A belief-effect paper should report placebo/balance tests on background beliefs to probe information equivalence.

In plain wordsA paper claiming an effect through beliefs should report placebo or balance tests showing whether background beliefs or attributes are similar across conditions.
Why it mattersThese tests check information equivalence; if background features differ, the result can no longer be read as a belief effect.
CheckWhether the paper reports a placebo / balance test on background beliefs or attributes to probe information equivalence.
PassThe paper reports a placebo or balance test on background beliefs/attributes bearing on information equivalence.
FlagThe paper claims a belief effect but reports no placebo/balance test on background beliefs.
Further reading
  • Allan Dafoe, Baobao Zhang, & Devin Caughey. (2018). Information Equivalence in Survey Experiments. Political Analysis, 26(4), 399-416. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2018.9, pp. 401, 404

Reporting2

What the paper must state for a reader to evaluate it.

SURVEY_EXP-REPORT-01severity if flagged: lowmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: conjointsilent: flag

A conjoint paper reporting AMCEs must state the attribute distribution used to average them.

In plain wordsA conjoint paper reporting AMCEs (average marginal component effects, the average effect of one attribute level) should state and justify the distribution of the other attributes used to average them.
Why it mattersAn AMCE depends on that distribution, and any target distribution should lie within the combinations the experiment actually used.
CheckWhether the paper states the distribution of the other attributes used to average its reported AMCEs.
PassThe paper states (and justifies) the distribution over the remaining attributes used to compute its AMCEs.
FlagThe paper reports AMCEs without stating the attribute distribution used to average them.
Further reading
  • Jens Hainmueller, Daniel J. Hopkins, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2013). Causal Inference in Conjoint Analysis: Understanding Multidimensional Choices via Stated Preference Experiments. Political Analysis, 22(1), 1-30. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpt024, p. 12
SURVEY_EXP-REPORT-02severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 7 sourcesapplies to: population_inferencesilent: flag

A paper generalizing to a population must state its sampling/weighting basis or an explicit generalization assumption.

In plain wordsA paper that generalizes its result to a population should state the basis: a probability sample with proper weights, or an explicit assumption it argues for, such as representativeness or similar effects across people.
Why it mattersHow a convenience sample relates to the population is generally unknown, and matching or reweighting on demographics does not settle it.
CheckWhether the paper that generalizes to a population states the basis for the generalization (probability sampling + weights, or an explicit assumption), rather than generalizing from a convenience/non-probability sample without justification.
PassThe paper states the basis for its population claim, a probability sample with appropriate weighting, or an explicit representativeness / effect-homogeneity assumption it argues for.
FlagThe paper generalizes its estimate to a population from a convenience or non-probability sample without stating a supporting assumption or design basis.
Further reading
  • Kevin Mullinix, Thomas J. Leeper, James Druckman, & Jeremy Freese. (2015). The Generalizability of Survey Experiments. Journal of Experimental Political Science, 2(2), 109-138. https://doi.org/10.1017/xps.2015.19, pp. 114, 123-124

Data2

Sample construction and measurement.

SURVEY_EXP-DATA-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 7 sourcesapplies to: list_experimentsilent: flag

A list-experiment paper should address ceiling and floor effects in its design or analysis.

In plain wordsA list-experiment paper should address ceiling and floor effects (respondents who would say yes to all or none of the items), through its choice of items or a correction or diagnostic.
Why it mattersThese effects bias the estimated share downward and remove the privacy the design relies on.
CheckWhether the paper addresses ceiling/floor effects, through control-item selection that avoids uniform response patterns or through a statistical correction or diagnostic.
PassThe paper addresses ceiling/floor effects, via control-item choice designed to avoid all-yes/all-no patterns, or via a correction or diagnostic for them.
FlagThe paper analyzes a list experiment without addressing ceiling or floor effects in design or analysis.
Further reading
  • Graeme Blair & Kosuke Imai. (2012). Statistical Analysis of List Experiments. Political Analysis, 20(1), 47-77. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpr048, pp. 66, 72
  • Kosuke Imai. (2011). Multivariate Regression Analysis for the Item Count Technique. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 106(494), 407-416. https://doi.org/10.1198/jasa.2011.ap10415, p. 408
SURVEY_EXP-DATA-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: framing_treatmentsilent: flag

A framing/wording experiment must include a control baseline or qualify conclusions when one is omitted.

In plain wordsA framing or wording experiment should include a no-treatment control group, or qualify its conclusions if it deliberately leaves one out.
Why it mattersWithout a control, a difference between frames cannot show which frame moved attitudes.
CheckWhether the design includes a no-treatment control/baseline condition, or, if it is deliberately omitted, whether the paper qualifies its conclusions.
PassThe paper includes a no-treatment control/baseline arm, or, where one is deliberately omitted, qualifies its conclusions accordingly.
FlagThe paper compares treatment/framing arms with no control baseline and does not qualify its conclusions.
Further reading
  • Brian J. Gaines, James H. Kuklinski, & Paul J. Quirk. (2006). The Logic of the Survey Experiment Reexamined. Political Analysis, 15(1), 1-20. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpl008, pp. 3, 9, 17
