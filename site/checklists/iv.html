The checklists

Instrumental variables

13 checks, each resting on claims from the methods literature. The 15 sources listed at the end are the ones cited as further reading. Each check says when it applies, what passes, what is flagged, and where to read more.

Identification 7Inference 6

How to read a verdict

Pass and flag are decided by the stated conditions. A check that is out of scope for the paper is marked does not apply; that is never a verdict on what the paper said. When a check applies and the paper says nothing, the verdict is the one given under silent.

A verdict covers only the text the audit could see. A flag on material the paper says is in an appendix or replication package means "not shown here", not "not done", and such flags carry that caveat in the report. Thresholds are rules of thumb unless marked otherwise, and near-threshold values are reported as borderline rather than rounded to a verdict.

When a check applies

A check applies only when its scope trigger fires. Triggers key on the paper's own explicit stance and default to out of scope when the paper is silent.

all
The paper states it estimates a causal effect of an endogenous regressor using an instrumental variable (2SLS/IV/GMM with an excluded instrument). Fires on any paper that names an instrument for an endogenous regressor. A paper that runs only OLS/regression adjustment and never names an instrument is out of scope.
as_if_random_or_conditional
Fires when the paper describes its instrument as randomly or as-if-randomly assigned (lottery/natural experiment), or as valid only conditional on named covariates. Near-miss that does NOT fire: an instrument defended purely by theoretical argument with no claim of (conditional) random assignment.
late_causal
The paper states it interprets its IV estimate as an average causal/treatment effect: it invokes potential outcomes or compliers/LATE, or it explicitly allows the treatment effect to vary across units (heterogeneous effects) and reads the 2SLS coefficient as such an average. Fires only on an explicit causal-effect reading. NEAR-MISS that does NOT fire: a paper that explicitly assumes a single constant/homogeneous structural coefficient and reports only that slope, making no complier/average-treatment-effect claim; a purely predictive/first-stage-only exercise. Ambiguity resolves OUT (NA): a coder never infers a causal-effect reading, or effect heterogeneity, the paper did not state.
multiple_endogenous
The paper states it instruments two or more endogenous regressors jointly in the same equation. Fires on a declared count >1 of endogenous regressors. NEAR-MISS: a single endogenous regressor with several instruments (overidentified but one endogenous variable) does NOT fire.

Identification7

The assumptions the causal claim rests on.

IV-IDENT-01severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

State the target causal parameter the IV estimate is meant to identify.

In plain wordsThe paper should state in words which causal effect its instrumental variable (IV) estimate is meant to capture and, if that effect applies only to a particular group, name that group.
Why it mattersWithout a stated target, readers cannot tell what quantity the estimate represents or whom it describes.
CheckWhether the paper contains an explicit statement naming the causal parameter being estimated (e.g., the effect of the endogenous regressor on the outcome for a named population).
PassThe paper names, in words, the causal parameter it estimates; where that parameter is population-specific (e.g., a local effect), it also identifies the population it applies to.
FlagThe paper reports an IV estimate without ever stating which causal parameter it is meant to identify.

IV-IDENT-02severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

Argue, substantively, why the instrument influences the outcome only through the endogenous regressor.

In plain wordsThe paper should give a reasoned argument, not a statistical test, for why the instrument affects the outcome only through the treatment (the exclusion restriction); a regression of the outcome on the instrument does not count.
Why it mattersThis assumption cannot be tested with the data, so the estimate is only as credible as the argument for it.
CheckWhether the paper presents reasoning (not a statistical test) defending that the instrument has no pathway to the outcome except through the treatment.
PassThe paper gives an explicit substantive argument for the exclusion restriction, findable anywhere in the paper or its appendices.
FlagThe paper invokes an instrument but offers no argued defense of the exclusion restriction, or defends it only by an OLS regression of the outcome on the instrument.
Further reading

IV-IDENT-03severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 7 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

The paper must argue the instrument is (as-if) independent of the outcome's unobserved determinants, explicitly when it is not physically randomized.

In plain wordsThe paper should argue that the instrument is unrelated to hidden factors that affect the outcome, as if randomly assigned, making this case explicitly when it was not actually randomized and naming any covariates it conditions on.
Why it mattersIV relies on this independence, and outside a true randomization it cannot simply be assumed.
CheckWhether the paper argues that the instrument is unrelated to outcome confounders / as-good-as-randomly assigned, and (when not physically randomized) makes that case rather than assuming it.
PassThe paper argues the instrument is independent of confounders of the treatment-outcome relationship, e.g. by establishing (conditional) random or as-if-random assignment and naming the conditioning covariates it relies on.
FlagThe paper treats a non-randomized instrument as exogenous without argument, or rests independence on an a-priori belief with no supporting reasoning.

IV-IDENT-04severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: as_if_random_or_conditionalsilent: flag

A paper claiming (conditional) random assignment must report a balance/randomization check of the instrument against predetermined covariates.

In plain wordsIf the paper claims the instrument is (conditionally) randomly assigned, it should check that the instrument is unrelated to characteristics fixed before assignment, using the same covariates as in its estimates.
Why it mattersRandom assignment implies the instrument should be unrelated to such characteristics, so this check tests the claim directly.
CheckWhether the paper reports comparing the instrument against variables fixed before assignment (a balance / randomization check), using the same covariate scheme it uses in estimation.
PassThe paper reports a balance/randomization check, e.g. tests or comparisons of predetermined covariates across instrument values, or an F-test for unexpected instrument-covariate correlations.
FlagThe paper claims (conditional) random or as-if-random assignment but reports no check of the instrument against predetermined covariates.

IV-IDENT-05severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: late_causalsilent: flag

Justify monotonicity (no defiers) when interpreting the IV estimand as an average treatment effect.

In plain wordsWhen the paper reads its IV estimate as an average treatment effect, it should justify, by argument or design, that the instrument pushes no unit's treatment in the opposite direction (no "defiers", also called monotonicity).
Why it mattersWithout this condition, the IV estimate cannot be read as an average effect when effects may vary across units.
CheckWhether the paper states and defends the monotonicity/no-defiers condition (by argument or design).
PassA paper with a fired late_causal trigger gives a theoretical or design-based justification that no defiers exist.
FlagA late_causal paper interprets the IV estimand as an average effect without addressing monotonicity.
Further reading
  • Joshua D. Angrist, Guido W. Imbens, & Donald B. Rubin. (1996). Identification of Causal Effects Using Instrumental Variables. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 91(434), 444-455. https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1996.10476902, p. 447

IV-IDENT-06severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

The paper must address no interference between units (SUTVA), by argument, design, or explicit modeling.

In plain wordsThe paper should address whether one unit's outcome could be affected by other units' instrument or treatment (interference, or spillovers), either by arguing it does not happen or by modeling it where expected.
Why it mattersIV assumes no interference between units, and spillovers between treated and untreated units bias the estimate.
CheckWhether the paper addresses no-interference / SUTVA -- by arguing that one unit's outcome is unaffected by other units' assignment or treatment, or by explicitly modeling spillovers where they are expected.
PassThe paper addresses no interference between units -- arguing from design or context that one unit's outcome is unaffected by other units' assignment/treatment, or explicitly modeling the interference where it is expected.
FlagThe paper neither argues no interference between units nor models spillovers -- it is silent on SUTVA.

IV-IDENT-07severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: late_causalsilent: flag

Acknowledge the local (complier) nature of the IV estimand and its limited generalizability.

In plain wordsWhen reading its IV estimate causally, the paper should acknowledge that it applies only to units whose treatment the instrument changes (compliers), not automatically to the whole population.
Why it mattersWhen effects vary across units, the effect for compliers need not equal the population effect, which limits how far the result generalizes.
CheckWhether the paper states that its IV estimate applies only to the subpopulation the instrument moves and flags the resulting limit on generalizability.
PassA fired late_causal paper states the locality of its estimand (compliers / instrument-shifted range) and does not silently generalize it to the whole population.
FlagA late_causal paper presents its IV estimate as a population/average effect with no acknowledgment of complier-locality.
Further reading

Inference6

Standard errors, tests, and their validity.

IV-INFER-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

Report a first-stage instrument-strength statistic (F or partial R-squared of the excluded instruments).

In plain wordsThe paper should report how strongly the instrument predicts the treatment in the first stage, using an F statistic or partial R-squared for the excluded instruments after accounting for covariates.
Why it mattersReaders need this statistic to judge whether the instrument is strong enough to support the IV estimate.
CheckWhether the paper reports a first-stage strength statistic for the excluded instrument(s).
PassThe paper reports a first-stage F statistic or partial R-squared of the excluded instruments (partialling out covariates), findable anywhere in the paper or appendices.
FlagThe paper reports IV/2SLS estimates but no first-stage strength statistic.
PrevalenceSurveys find IV papers routinely omit any first-stage relevance statistic (Staiger-Stock 1997; Sovey 2010).
DisputedAndrews (2019) argues first-stage F-statistics can be spuriously large even with irrelevant instruments and so may not reliably indicate whether conventional inference performs well, and Olea (2013) argues the ordinary and robust first-stage F cannot substitute for the effective F.
Further reading
  • John Bound, David A. Jaeger, & Regina Baker. (1995). Problems with Instrumental Variables Estimation When the Correlation Between the Instruments and the Endogeneous Explanatory Variable is Weak. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 90(430), 443. https://doi.org/10.2307/2291055, pp. 443-444
  • Douglas O. Staiger & James H. Stock. (1997). Instrumental Variables Regression with Weak Instruments. Econometrica, 65(3), 557. https://doi.org/10.2307/2171753, p. 582
IV-INFER-02severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

Use identification-robust inference when the first stage is weak.

In plain wordsIf the instrument is weak or borderline (an effective F below 23.1), the paper should add inference that stays valid with weak instruments, such as an Anderson-Rubin confidence set, rather than relying only on conventional standard errors.
Why it mattersWith a weak first stage, conventional confidence intervals contain the true value less often than claimed, and rescaling standard errors does not fix this.
CheckWhether, given a weak first stage, the paper reports weak-instrument-robust inference rather than relying only on conventional 2SLS standard errors.
PassEither the first-stage effective F is at or above the strong-instrument threshold (23.1, the conservative worst-case value for TSLS) and conventional inference is reported; or the effective F is weak/borderline and the paper additionally reports weak-instrument-robust inference (e.g., an Anderson-Rubin confidence set).
FlagThe first-stage effective F is below the strong-instrument threshold and the paper reports only conventional t/Wald standard errors or CIs, with no identification-robust inference.
Thresholdcutoff 10; provenance: rule of thumb; borderline band: [10, 23.1]
Inside the bandJudge strength on the first-stage EFFECTIVE F (not an ordinary F against homoskedastic critical values; see IV-INFER-03). At or above 23.1 (the conservative worst-case effective-F critical value for TSLS) conventional inference alone PASSes; below 10 the first stage is weak and a weak-instrument-robust confidence set is required to PASS; within [10, 23.1] strength is not established at the conservative bound, so PASS only if a weak-instrument-robust confidence set is also reported, otherwise FLAG.
Further reading
  • Isaiah Andrews, James H. Stock, & Liyang Sun. (2019). Weak Instruments in Instrumental Variables Regression: Theory and Practice. Annual Review of Economics, 11(1), 727-753. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-080218-025643, p. 742
  • José Luis Montiel Olea & Carolin Pflueger. (2013). A Robust Test for Weak Instruments. Journal of Business and Economic Statistics, 31(3), 358-369. https://doi.org/10.1080/00401706.2013.806694, p. 358

  • James H. Stock, Jonathan H. Wright, & Motohiro Yogo. (2002). A Survey of Weak Instruments and Weak Identification in Generalized Method of Moments. Journal of Business and Economic Statistics, 20(4), 518-529. https://doi.org/10.1198/073500102288618658, p. 519
IV-INFER-03severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

Assess instrument strength with an error-structure-matched robust statistic (effective F), not a plain F against homoskedastic critical values.

In plain wordsWhen errors are clustered, correlated over time, or uneven in spread, the paper should judge instrument strength with a statistic that accounts for this (the effective F), not an ordinary F compared to standard Stock-Yogo cutoffs.
Why it mattersThose standard cutoffs assume errors of even spread, so applying them under other error patterns can misjudge whether the instrument is strong enough.
CheckWhether the strength diagnostic the paper uses matches its error structure (effective / robust weak-instrument statistic) rather than an ordinary F judged against homoskedastic critical values.
PassWith non-homoskedastic/clustered/serial errors, the paper assesses strength with a heteroskedasticity/HAC/cluster-robust weak-instrument statistic (e.g., effective F).
FlagThe paper has non-homoskedastic/clustered/serial errors but judges strength by an ordinary (or plain robust) first-stage F against homoskedastic Stock-Yogo values.
Further reading
  • Isaiah Andrews, James H. Stock, & Liyang Sun. (2019). Weak Instruments in Instrumental Variables Regression: Theory and Practice. Annual Review of Economics, 11(1), 727-753. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-080218-025643, pp. 737-738
  • José Luis Montiel Olea & Carolin Pflueger. (2013). A Robust Test for Weak Instruments. Journal of Business and Economic Statistics, 31(3), 358-369. https://doi.org/10.1080/00401706.2013.806694, p. 365
IV-INFER-04severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: pass

Do not screen/select specifications by a first-stage F threshold.

In plain wordsThe paper should not keep or drop specifications, samples, or instruments based on whether their first-stage F statistic passes a cutoff.
Why it mattersScreening on the F statistic biases the estimates that survive and distorts the error rates of the tests.
CheckWhether the paper reports keeping/dropping specifications, samples, or instruments on the basis of a first-stage F cutoff.
PassThe paper does not screen specifications on a first-stage F threshold (silence about such screening passes).
FlagThe paper states it retained only specifications, samples, or instruments whose first-stage F exceeded a cutoff.
Further reading
  • Isaiah Andrews, James H. Stock, & Liyang Sun. (2019). Weak Instruments in Instrumental Variables Regression: Theory and Practice. Annual Review of Economics, 11(1), 727-753. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-080218-025643, pp. 740-741
IV-INFER-05severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

Use a variance estimator matched to the error/clustering structure and the two-stage design.

In plain wordsThe paper should compute standard errors suited to the data's structure, for example clustering at the level where observations are related or correcting for correlation over time, and account for the two estimation stages.
Why it mattersStandard errors that ignore this structure or the two-stage estimation can misstate uncertainty, so the reported IV standard errors may not be valid.
CheckWhether the reported IV standard errors use a variance estimator appropriate to the design's heteroskedasticity/clustering/serial-correlation and account for the estimation stages.
PassThe paper reports standard errors from a variance estimator matched to its error structure (e.g., clustered at the dependence level; HAC for time series) and consistent with the two-stage/generated-regressor structure.
FlagThe paper uses default/homoskedastic or mismatched-level standard errors where the design implies clustering or serial correlation, or ignores the two-stage estimation.
Further reading

IV-INFER-06severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: multiple_endogenoussilent: flag

Assess joint identification with a Cragg-Donald/conditional-F statistic under multiple endogenous regressors.

In plain wordsWith more than one treatment variable needing an instrument, the paper should assess joint instrument strength with a statistic built for that case, such as Cragg-Donald or conditional (Sanderson-Windmeijer) F, rather than each first stage's own F.
Why it mattersSeparate first-stage F statistics are not designed to show whether the instruments jointly identify the effects of all the treatments.
CheckWhether a paper with multiple endogenous regressors reports a joint/conditional identification statistic rather than only per-equation F statistics.
PassA fired multiple_endogenous paper reports a Cragg-Donald and/or conditional (Sanderson-Windmeijer) first-stage statistic.
FlagA multiple_endogenous paper judges strength only from separate per-equation first-stage F statistics.
