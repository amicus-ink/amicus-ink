The checklists

Conjoint experiments

14 checks, each resting on claims from the methods literature. The 11 sources listed at the end are the ones cited as further reading. Each check says when it applies, what passes, what is flagged, and where to read more.

How to read a verdict

Pass and flag are decided by the stated conditions. A check that is out of scope for the paper is marked does not apply; that is never a verdict on what the paper said. When a check applies and the paper says nothing, the verdict is the one given under silent.

A verdict covers only the text the audit could see. A flag on material the paper says is in an appendix or replication package means "not shown here", not "not done", and such flags carry that caveat in the report. Thresholds are rules of thumb unless marked otherwise, and near-threshold values are reported as borderline rather than rounded to a verdict.

When a check applies

A check applies only when its scope trigger fires. Triggers key on the paper's own explicit stance and default to out of scope when the paper is silent.

all
Any study that fields a conjoint or factorial survey experiment and reports attribute-level effects (AMCEs, marginal means, or equivalent regression coefficients on attribute levels).
restricted_randomization
Fires when the paper states it restricted, excluded, or linked attribute combinations (conditional / non-independent randomization). Does NOT fire when the paper states every attribute was assigned fully independently with no combination excluded.
subgroup_comparison
Fires when the paper states it estimates attribute effects separately for two or more respondent subgroups (conditional AMCEs) and compares them. Does NOT fire for a paper reporting only pooled AMCEs, nor for a robustness subset of tasks/profiles (a respondent subgroup is required).
repeated_tasks
Fires when the paper states each respondent completed more than one choice or rating task. Does NOT fire for a design that assigns one task per respondent, and is not fired merely by a large respondent count.
data_driven_selection
Fires when the paper states it uses a data-driven procedure (regularization, machine learning, model/level selection) to collapse factor levels or to define respondent subgroups that are then used in estimation. Does NOT fire for pre-specified subgroups or a standard F-test for interactions.
population_target
Fires when the paper states its AMCE is meant to represent a real-world or population profile distribution that differs from the (typically uniform / independent) distribution used in its own randomization. Does NOT fire for a paper that interprets its effects only with respect to its own experimental randomization distribution.
fractional_factorial
Fires when the paper states it uses a fractional factorial, D-optimal, or orthogonal-array design (a chosen subset of profile combinations). Does NOT fire when the paper states it assigned each attribute by independent per-attribute randomization.

Identification3

The assumptions the causal claim rests on.

CONJOINT-IDENT-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 6 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A conjoint paper must state that its attribute levels were randomly and (conditionally) independently assigned across profiles.

In plain wordsThe paper should state that the levels of each attribute (a feature of the profiles respondents compare, such as a candidate's age) were assigned at random and independently, or conditionally independently, of the other attributes.
Why it mattersThis random, independent assignment is what lets the paper recover each attribute's effect without relying on a statistical model's assumptions.
CheckWhether the paper reports that attribute levels were randomly assigned, and (conditionally) independently of one another, across profiles.
PassThe paper states that attribute levels were randomly assigned and that the assignment was independent (or conditionally independent) of the other attributes.
FlagThe paper reports attribute-level effects without stating that attribute levels were randomly and (conditionally) independently assigned.
Further reading
  • Kirk Bansak, Jens Hainmueller, Daniel J. Hopkins, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2022). Using Conjoint Experiments to Analyze Election Outcomes: The Essential Role of the Average Marginal Component Effect. Political Analysis, 31(4), 500-518. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2022.16, p. 514
  • Jens Hainmueller & Daniel J. Hopkins. (2014). The Hidden American Immigration Consensus: A Conjoint Analysis of Attitudes toward Immigrants. American Journal of Political Science, 59(3), 529-548. https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12138, p. 537
  • Dawn Langan Teele, Joshua Kalla, & Frances Rosenbluth. (2018). The Ties That Double Bind: Social Roles and Women's Underrepresentation in Politics. American Political Science Review, 112(3), 525-541. https://doi.org/10.1017/s0003055418000217, p. 530
CONJOINT-IDENT-02severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: restricted_randomizationsilent: flag

A paper that restricts attribute combinations must confine the affected effects to the permitted strata and not present them as spanning the full profile space.

In plain wordsIf the design rules out some combinations of attributes, the paper should limit the affected effects to the combinations that were allowed, or state that excluded combinations cannot be analyzed without added assumptions.
Why it mattersEffects for those attributes are defined only over the allowed combinations, so presenting them as covering every possible profile claims more than the data can show.
CheckWhether, given a stated restriction on attribute combinations, the paper limits or qualifies the affected effects to the permitted strata rather than reporting them over the full profile space.
PassFor attributes touched by the restriction, the paper reports the effect as an average over the permitted strata of the linked attribute, or states that excluded combinations cannot be analyzed without added assumptions.
FlagThe paper reports AMCEs for restricted attributes as if they ranged over the full profile space, with no acknowledgement that the restriction confines the estimand to permitted strata.
Further reading
  • Jens Hainmueller, Daniel J. Hopkins, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2013). Causal Inference in Conjoint Analysis: Understanding Multidimensional Choices via Stated Preference Experiments. Political Analysis, 22(1), 1-30. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpt024, pp. 9, 20
  • Jens Hainmueller & Daniel J. Hopkins. (2014). The Hidden American Immigration Consensus: A Conjoint Analysis of Attitudes toward Immigrants. American Journal of Political Science, 59(3), 529-548. https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12138, p. 537
CONJOINT-IDENT-03severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: subgroup_comparisonsilent: flag

Variables used to condition or subset attribute effects must be established as pre-treatment or argued to be unaffected by the treatment.

In plain wordsWhen the paper splits or conditions attribute effects by a respondent characteristic, it should report that the characteristic was measured before the conjoint tasks or argue that the tasks could not have affected it.
Why it mattersA characteristic changed by the experiment itself can distort the comparison, a problem called post-treatment bias.
CheckWhether the paper reports that the conditioning/moderator variable was measured before the conjoint tasks, or explicitly argues the treatment cannot have affected it.
PassThe paper reports pre-treatment measurement of the conditioning variable, or gives a stated argument that the treatment cannot affect it.
FlagThe paper conditions attribute effects on a respondent characteristic without stating it is pre-treatment or arguing it is unaffected by the treatment.
Further reading
  • Jens Hainmueller, Daniel J. Hopkins, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2013). Causal Inference in Conjoint Analysis: Understanding Multidimensional Choices via Stated Preference Experiments. Political Analysis, 22(1), 1-30. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpt024, p. 13
  • Dawn Langan Teele, Joshua Kalla, & Frances Rosenbluth. (2018). The Ties That Double Bind: Social Roles and Women's Underrepresentation in Politics. American Political Science Review, 112(3), 525-541. https://doi.org/10.1017/s0003055418000217, p. 531

Estimation5

Whether the estimator recovers the target parameter.

CONJOINT-EST-01severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: allsilent: flag

A conjoint paper must name the causal quantity of interest it maps to a well-defined estimand.

In plain wordsThe paper should name the causal quantity its conjoint estimates are meant to recover, such as the AMCE (average marginal component effect, the average effect of changing one attribute level) for a stated contrast.
Why it mattersWithout a named target, readers cannot tell what the reported estimates actually represent.
CheckWhether the paper names the causal quantity or estimand its conjoint estimates are meant to recover (e.g., the AMCE for a stated attribute contrast).
PassThe paper names the causal quantity / estimand its conjoint estimates are meant to recover.
FlagThe paper reports conjoint estimates without stating what causal quantity or estimand they are meant to represent.
Further reading
  • Kirk Bansak, Jens Hainmueller, Daniel J. Hopkins, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2022). Using Conjoint Experiments to Analyze Election Outcomes: The Essential Role of the Average Marginal Component Effect. Political Analysis, 31(4), 500-518. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2022.16, pp. 502, 512
CONJOINT-EST-02severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: pass

A paper must not interpret an AMCE as a majority preference or as the fraction of respondents preferring a level.

In plain wordsThe paper should not read an AMCE (the average effect of changing one attribute level) as showing that a majority, or some stated share, of respondents prefer that level.
Why it mattersThe AMCE combines which way respondents lean with how strongly they lean, so it cannot reveal what share of them prefer the level.
CheckWhether the paper claims that an AMCE shows a majority of respondents prefer a level, or reports it as the fraction/proportion of respondents (or voters) preferring a level.
PassThe paper makes no majority-preference or fraction/proportion-preferring claim on the basis of an AMCE.
FlagThe paper interprets an AMCE as showing that a majority, or a stated fraction/proportion, of respondents prefer the attribute level.
PrevalenceDirection and intensity of preferences are empirically correlated on most issues (Abramson 2022), so the failure mode is realistic rather than pathological.
Further reading
  • Scott F Abramson, Korhan Koçak, & Asya Magazinnik. (2022). What Do We Learn about Voter Preferences from Conjoint Experiments?. American Journal of Political Science, 66(4), 1008-1020. https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12714, pp. 1017-1018
  • Kirk Bansak, Jens Hainmueller, Daniel J. Hopkins, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2022). Using Conjoint Experiments to Analyze Election Outcomes: The Essential Role of the Average Marginal Component Effect. Political Analysis, 31(4), 500-518. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2022.16, p. 504
CONJOINT-EST-03severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: pass

A paper must not interpret an AMCE as a probability of winning or a statement of relative electability.

In plain wordsThe paper should not present an AMCE (the average effect of changing one attribute level) as an effect on a profile's chance of winning an election or beating a specific opponent, or as a claim about its relative electability.
Why it mattersAMCE estimates do not identify how an attribute changes a candidate's probability of winning or electability.
CheckWhether the paper interprets an AMCE as an effect on the probability of winning an election, the likelihood of being elected, the chance of beating a specific opponent, or the relative electability of a profile.
PassThe paper makes no probability-of-winning, beat-a-specific-opponent, or electability claim on the basis of an AMCE.
FlagThe paper interprets an AMCE as an effect on the probability of winning, the likelihood of election, the chance of beating a specific baseline opponent, or the relative electability of a candidate/profile.
Further reading
  • Kirk Bansak, Jens Hainmueller, Daniel J. Hopkins, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2022). Using Conjoint Experiments to Analyze Election Outcomes: The Essential Role of the Average Marginal Component Effect. Political Analysis, 31(4), 500-518. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2022.16, pp. 509, 511
  • Scott F Abramson, Korhan Koçak, & Asya Magazinnik. (2022). What Do We Learn about Voter Preferences from Conjoint Experiments?. American Journal of Political Science, 66(4), 1008-1020. https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12714, p. 1018
CONJOINT-EST-04severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: pass

A paper must not present conjoint AMCEs as predictions of absolute outcome levels or real-world aggregate outcomes.

In plain wordsThe paper should treat its conjoint estimates as relative effects of attributes, not as predictions of absolute levels, such as an acceptance rate, or of real-world totals, such as an election result.
Why it mattersConjoint estimates capture relative attribute effects only; in a forced-choice design, where respondents must pick one profile, the overall selection rate is fixed by the design.
CheckWhether the paper reads its conjoint estimates as predicting an absolute level (e.g., an acceptance/rejection rate) or a real-world aggregate outcome (e.g., an election result).
PassThe paper interprets its estimates as relative attribute effects and does not present them as absolute levels or real-world outcome predictions.
FlagThe paper interprets AMCEs as predicting an absolute outcome level or a real-world aggregate outcome.
Further reading
  • Jens Hainmueller, Dominik Hangartner, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2015). Validating vignette and conjoint survey experiments against real-world behavior. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 112(8), 2395-2400. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1416587112, p. 2399
  • Yusaku Horiuchi, Daniel M. Smith, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2018). Measuring Voters’ Multidimensional Policy Preferences with Conjoint Analysis: Application to Japan’s 2014 Election. Political Analysis, 26(2), 190-209. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2018.2, p. 194
CONJOINT-EST-05severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 4 sourcesapplies to: subgroup_comparisonsilent: pass

A paper must not read differences in conditional AMCEs across subgroups as differences in subgroup preferences.

In plain wordsThe paper should not read differences or similarities in AMCEs across respondent subgroups as differences or similarities in preferences, unless it bases the comparison on marginal means (how often profiles with a level are chosen) or another baseline-free quantity.
Why it mattersSubgroup AMCEs depend on the arbitrary choice of baseline level, so differences between them need not reflect differences in preferences.
CheckWhether the paper describes a difference in preferences between respondent subgroups on the basis of a difference in their conditional AMCEs, without reporting a reference-invariant quantity such as marginal means.
PassThe paper bases any subgroup preference comparison on marginal means or another reference-invariant quantity, or makes no subgroup preference-difference claim.
FlagThe paper interprets a difference (or similarity) in conditional AMCEs across subgroups as a difference (or similarity) in subgroup preferences.
Further reading
  • Thomas J. Leeper, Sara B. Hobolt, & James Tilley. (2019). Measuring Subgroup Preferences in Conjoint Experiments. Political Analysis, 28(2), 207-221. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2019.30, pp. 208, 230

Inference2

Standard errors, tests, and their validity.

CONJOINT-INFER-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: repeated_taskssilent: flag

A repeated-task conjoint must report inference that accounts for within-respondent clustering.

In plain wordsWhen each respondent completes several conjoint tasks, the paper should report standard errors or confidence intervals that account for this, for example by clustering on the respondent or resampling whole respondents (a block bootstrap).
Why it mattersA respondent's answers across tasks are related, so default standard errors are substantially biased.
CheckWhether the paper reports respondent-clustered standard errors, a respondent-level block bootstrap, or an equivalent within-respondent correction.
PassThe paper reports standard errors/confidence intervals clustered on the respondent, a respondent-level block bootstrap, or an equivalent adjustment for within-respondent dependence.
FlagThe paper reports standard errors/confidence intervals with no adjustment for the dependence among a respondent's repeated tasks.
Further reading
  • Jens Hainmueller, Daniel J. Hopkins, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2013). Causal Inference in Conjoint Analysis: Understanding Multidimensional Choices via Stated Preference Experiments. Political Analysis, 22(1), 1-30. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpt024, p. 17
  • Jens Hainmueller & Daniel J. Hopkins. (2014). The Hidden American Immigration Consensus: A Conjoint Analysis of Attitudes toward Immigrants. American Journal of Political Science, 59(3), 529-548. https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12138, p. 537
CONJOINT-INFER-02severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: data_driven_selectionsilent: flag

Data-driven level or subgroup selection must be separated from effect estimation by a sample split.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses a data-driven method, such as regularization or machine learning, to choose which levels to combine or which subgroups to study, it should make that choice on one part of the data and estimate effects on a separate part.
Why it mattersSelecting and estimating on the same data can bias the resulting estimates.
CheckWhether the paper reports splitting its data so that the data-driven selection and the effect estimation use separate samples.
PassThe paper reports using separate training and estimation samples (cross-fitting / a train-test split) for the data-driven selection.
FlagThe paper uses a data-driven procedure to select levels or subgroups and estimates effects on the same data without any sample split.
Further reading
  • Brandon De La Cuesta, Naoki Egami, & Kosuke Imai. (2021). Improving the External Validity of Conjoint Analysis: The Essential Role of Profile Distribution. Political Analysis, 30(1), 19-45. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2020.40, p. 36
  • Yusaku Horiuchi, Zachary Markovich, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2021). Does Conjoint Analysis Mitigate Social Desirability Bias?. Political Analysis, 30(4), 535-549. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2021.30, p. 545

Robustness1

Sensitivity, diagnostics, and alternative specifications.

CONJOINT-ROBUST-01severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: population_targetsilent: flag

A paper targeting a non-uniform profile distribution must report AMCE estimates under two or more profile distributions.

In plain wordsIf the paper's target profile distribution (how often each combination of attribute levels appears) is non-uniform and differs from the one used in randomization, the paper should report AMCE estimates under two or more distributions, including its stated target.
Why it mattersAn AMCE from uniform randomization carries over to a different target only if attributes do not interact, so readers need to see how estimates change.
CheckWhether the paper reports AMCE estimates under more than one profile distribution when its stated target distribution differs from its randomization distribution.
PassThe paper reports its AMCE estimates under two or more distinct profile distributions, including the distribution it names as its target.
FlagThe paper states a non-uniform target for its AMCE but reports estimates under only its single (e.g., uniform) randomization distribution.
Further reading
  • Brandon De La Cuesta, Naoki Egami, & Kosuke Imai. (2021). Improving the External Validity of Conjoint Analysis: The Essential Role of Profile Distribution. Political Analysis, 30(1), 19-45. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2020.40, pp. 21-22, 34

Reporting2

What the paper must state for a reader to evaluate it.

CONJOINT-REPORT-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

The paper states and justifies the weighting/target profile distribution used for its AMCEs.

In plain wordsThe paper should state the profile distribution (how much weight each combination of attribute levels gets, for example equal weight) over which its AMCEs are averaged, and justify that choice.
Why it mattersAn AMCE averages over the other attributes, so its value depends on the distribution used.
CheckWhether the paper states the weighting/target profile distribution over which its AMCEs are averaged and gives a rationale for it.
PassThe paper states the weighting/target profile distribution used for its AMCEs (e.g. uniform, or a stated target) and gives a justification for that choice.
FlagThe paper reports AMCEs without stating the weighting/target distribution used, or states it but offers no justification.
Further reading
  • Brandon De La Cuesta, Naoki Egami, & Kosuke Imai. (2021). Improving the External Validity of Conjoint Analysis: The Essential Role of Profile Distribution. Political Analysis, 30(1), 19-45. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2020.40, pp. 20-21
  • Jens Hainmueller, Daniel J. Hopkins, & Teppei Yamamoto. (2013). Causal Inference in Conjoint Analysis: Understanding Multidimensional Choices via Stated Preference Experiments. Political Analysis, 22(1), 1-30. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpt024, p. 12
CONJOINT-REPORT-02severity if flagged: lowhigh consensus, 5 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

The paper acknowledges the external-validity limits of its conjoint design.

In plain wordsThe paper should acknowledge at least one limit on how far its conjoint results generalize, such as applying only to the levels tested, profiles that may be unrealistic, or a sample that may not match the target population.
Why it mattersConjoint results do not automatically carry over to the real world; they depend on the levels included, the realism of the profiles and the sample.
CheckWhether the paper acknowledges at least one core external-validity limit of its conjoint (interpretation confined to the included levels, profile realism, or sample-to-target correspondence).
PassThe paper acknowledges an external-validity limit of its design (e.g. that estimates apply only to the levels tested, that profiles may be unrealistic, or that the sample may not match the target population).
FlagThe paper generalizes its conjoint estimates to real-world targets or populations without acknowledging any external-validity limit.
If flagged, the author can fix it byState the external-validity limits of your conjoint: that estimates are preferences over the specific attribute levels included (not the universe of possible positions), whether the profiles are realistic relative to real-world targets, and that results may not transfer to other contexts or to real behavior. Do not let readers infer that AMCEs generalize automatically.

Data1

Sample construction and measurement.

CONJOINT-DATA-01severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: fractional_factorialsilent: flag

A fractional factorial conjoint design must report orthogonality (within-alternative for unlabeled designs) and level balance.

In plain wordsIf the paper uses a fractional factorial design (showing only a subset of all attribute combinations), it should report that the design is orthogonal, meaning attributes are uncorrelated (for generic options such as Candidate A and Candidate B, within each option is enough), and balanced, meaning each level appears equally often.
Why it mattersBoth properties are needed for efficient estimation that does not mix up one attribute's effect with another's.
CheckWhether a paper that declares a fractional factorial / optimal design reports that the design is orthogonal (within-alternative orthogonality suffices for unlabeled designs) and that its attribute levels are balanced.
PassThe paper reports that its fractional factorial design is orthogonal (within-alternative orthogonality suffices for unlabeled/generic-profile designs) and that its attribute levels are balanced. Reporting only one property flags for the missing property.
FlagThe paper declares a fractional factorial design but does not report that it is orthogonal (within-alternative for unlabeled designs), or does not report that its attribute levels are balanced.
Further reading
  • Fabio Franchino & Francesco Zucchini. (2014). Voting in a Multi-dimensional Space: A Conjoint Analysis Employing Valence and Ideology Attributes of Candidates. Political Science Research and Methods, 3(2), 221-241. https://doi.org/10.1017/psrm.2014.24, p. 226
