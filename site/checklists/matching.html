The checklists

Matching

12 checks, each resting on claims from the methods literature. The 10 sources listed at the end are the ones cited as further reading. Each check says when it applies, what passes, what is flagged, and where to read more.

How to read a verdict

Pass and flag are decided by the stated conditions. A check that is out of scope for the paper is marked does not apply; that is never a verdict on what the paper said. When a check applies and the paper says nothing, the verdict is the one given under silent.

A verdict covers only the text the audit could see. A flag on material the paper says is in an appendix or replication package means "not shown here", not "not done", and such flags carry that caveat in the report. Thresholds are rules of thumb unless marked otherwise, and near-threshold values are reported as borderline rather than rounded to a verdict.

When a check applies

A check applies only when its scope trigger fires. Triggers key on the paper's own explicit stance and default to out of scope when the paper is silent.

all
Any observational or design-based study that reports estimating a treatment/exposure effect by matching, subclassification, or covariate-balancing weighting.
prunes_units
Fires only if the paper explicitly states it drops, prunes, discards, or restricts units in a way that changes which units the reported effect is estimated for -- e.g. it names a restriction to a common-support/overlap region, or says it drops treated units that have no acceptable match. Does NOT fire when the paper reuses controls via matching-with-replacement but keeps all treated units, and does NOT fire when the only exclusion is data cleaning such as dropping rows with missing covariates.
interference
Fires only if the paper explicitly states that units interfere -- that one unit's treatment can affect another unit's outcome (spillover, contagion, peer/network effects, an exposure mapping). Does NOT fire merely because data are clustered/grouped or geographically arranged, and does NOT fire for a paper that explicitly assumes SUTVA/no-interference.
continuous_treatment
Fires only if the paper states its treatment/exposure is continuous or dose-valued rather than binary. Does NOT fire when the treatment is binary but the estimated propensity score is continuous, and does NOT fire when the paper explicitly defines and analyzes a binary treatment even if an underlying dose exists.
multi_continuous_matching
Fires when the paper states it matches directly on two or more continuously measured covariates (not solely on a one-dimensional propensity score). Near-miss: matching on a single propensity score, or on discrete/categorical cells only, does not fire.
weighting
Fires only if the paper states it forms effect estimates using inverse-probability, propensity-score, or covariate-balancing weights. Does NOT fire for propensity-score pair/nearest-neighbor matching that produces matched sets rather than weights, and does NOT fire for plain outcome regression.
missing_data
Fires only if the paper states that some covariate (or outcome) values used for matching are missing. Does NOT fire for a study that reports complete data on all matching variables.

Identification5

The assumptions the causal claim rests on.

MATCHING-IDENT-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 8 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

The paper must state the selection-on-observables assumption it relies on and the covariates meant to satisfy it.

In plain wordsThe paper should state that its causal estimate assumes treatment is as good as random once observed covariates are accounted for (often called unconfoundedness or selection on observables), and name the covariates meant to make that hold.
Why it mattersA matched or adjusted estimate is causal only under this assumption, so readers need to see it and the covariates behind it.
CheckWhether the paper names the identifying assumption (unconfoundedness, conditional independence of treatment and potential outcomes given covariates, ignorability, or selection on observables) and points to the covariates intended to make it hold.
PassThe paper explicitly states that identification rests on conditioning on observed covariates (using a term such as unconfoundedness, ignorability, conditional independence, or selection on observables) and indicates which covariates carry that assumption.
FlagThe paper reports a matched or adjusted causal estimate without stating any conditional-independence / selection-on-observables assumption behind it.
Further reading

MATCHING-IDENT-02severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 6 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A matching paper must address the overlap / common-support condition under which comparable controls exist for the treated units.

In plain wordsThe paper should state or check that treated units have comparable control units with similar covariate values (called overlap or common support).
Why it mattersMatching can only find fair comparisons where treated and control units actually resemble each other; for an effect on the treated, it is enough that treated units fall within the range of the controls.
CheckWhether the paper states or checks that treated units have comparable controls in covariate space (a common-support / overlap condition, or that treated support lies within control support).
PassThe paper explicitly discusses overlap or common support -- e.g. it states the treated covariate support lies inside the control support, restricts to a region of common support, or otherwise asserts comparable controls exist for the treated units.
FlagThe paper matches and reports an effect without any statement or check that the treated units have comparable controls in covariate space.
Further reading
  • Alberto Abadie & Jann Spiess. (2020). Robust Post-Matching Inference. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 117(538), 983-995. https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2020.1840383, p. 985

MATCHING-IDENT-03severity if flagged: mediumhigh consensus, 3 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A matching paper must state SUTVA -- no interference and an identically-defined treatment -- as an assumption behind its estimate.

In plain wordsThe paper should state that one unit's outcome is not affected by other units' treatment and that the treatment means the same thing for every unit (together called SUTVA).
Why it mattersThe matched comparison estimates a well-defined effect only when this assumption holds.
CheckWhether the paper states SUTVA or its components -- no interference between units and a consistently defined treatment.
PassThe paper explicitly invokes SUTVA, or states its content: that one unit's outcome is unaffected by other units' assignments and that the treatment means the same thing across units (for a non-binary treatment, that potential outcomes are defined on the original treatment).
FlagThe paper never states the no-interference / stable-treatment assumption underlying its matched contrast.
Further reading
  • Christian Fong, Chad Hazlett, & Kosuke Imai. (2018). Covariate balancing propensity score for a continuous treatment: Application to the efficacy of political advertisements. The Annals of Applied Statistics, 12(1). https://doi.org/10.1214/17-aoas1101, p. 160
  • Jasjeet S. Sekhon. (2011). Multivariate and Propensity Score Matching Software with Automated Balance Optimization: The<b>Matching</b>Package for<i>R</i>. Journal of Statistical Software, 42(7). https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v042.i07, p. 4 (PDF pagination)
MATCHING-IDENT-04severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: prunes_unitssilent: flag

When pruning changes which units the effect is defined over, the paper must characterize the redefined estimand.

In plain wordsWhen the paper drops units, such as unmatched treated units, so the effect no longer covers the original population, it should say which units the reported effect now applies to.
Why it mattersReaders need to know what group the estimate actually describes, since it may differ from the population first targeted.
CheckFor a paper that restricts to a feasible/common-support region or drops unmatched treated units: whether it states which units the reported effect is now defined over.
PassThe paper explicitly characterizes the redefined estimand -- it says the effect now applies to the retained/matched subset (e.g. a feasible-sample ATT/ATE), and describes or depicts which units that is.
FlagThe paper prunes units in an estimand-changing way but still reports the effect as if it applied to the original target population, without characterizing the retained set.
Further reading
  • Stefano M. Iacus, Gary King, & Giuseppe Porro. (2011). Causal Inference without Balance Checking: Coarsened Exact Matching. Political Analysis, 20(1), 1-24. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpr013, pp. 5, 23
  • Gary King & Richard A. Nielsen. (2019). Why Propensity Scores Should Not Be Used for Matching. Political Analysis, 27(4), 435-454. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2019.11, p. 437
MATCHING-IDENT-05severity if flagged: highlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: interferencesilent: flag

A paper that declares interference must specify an exposure mapping and the exposure-specific estimand it targets.

In plain wordsWhen the paper says units affect each other (interference or spillover), it should give a clear rule for how everyone's treatment translates into each unit's exposure, and name the exposure-specific effect it estimates.
Why it mattersWith spillovers, a simple treated-or-not indicator no longer defines what each unit experienced, so the estimate is unclear without such a rule.
CheckFor a paper that declares interference/spillover: whether it specifies an exposure mapping (how assignments translate into each unit's exposure) and the exposure-specific quantity it estimates.
PassThe paper states an explicit exposure mapping constraining the interference (e.g. exposure defined by own treatment plus a summary of neighbors' treatments) and names the exposure-specific effect/estimand it targets.
FlagThe paper declares spillover/interference but estimates effects from a bare treatment indicator with no exposure mapping, leaving what exposure the estimate refers to unspecified.
Further reading
  • Peter M. Aronow & Cyrus Samii. (2017). Estimating average causal effects under general interference, with application to a social network experiment. The Annals of Applied Statistics, 11(4). https://doi.org/10.1214/16-aoas1005, pp. 2, 5, 28 (PDF pagination)

Estimation1

Whether the estimator recovers the target parameter.

MATCHING-EST-01severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: continuous_treatmentsilent: pass

A paper with a declared-continuous treatment must not dichotomize it for the causal estimate.

In plain wordsWhen the paper describes its treatment as continuous, like a dose, it should estimate the effect on that continuous scale rather than splitting it into treated and untreated.
Why it mattersSplitting a dose into two groups throws away information, can leave hidden imbalance in dose that biases the estimate, and the result cannot be read as the cutoff dose versus zero.
CheckFor a paper whose treatment it declares continuous/dose-valued: whether it collapses that treatment into a binary indicator for its causal estimate.
PassThe paper estimates the effect on the original continuous treatment scale (e.g. a dose-response / generalized-propensity-score analysis), not on a binarized version.
FlagThe paper dichotomizes its declared-continuous treatment into treated/untreated for the causal estimate (and, a fortiori, interprets that estimate as a fixed-dose-versus-zero contrast).
Further reading
  • Christian Fong, Chad Hazlett, & Kosuke Imai. (2018). Covariate balancing propensity score for a continuous treatment: Application to the efficacy of political advertisements. The Annals of Applied Statistics, 12(1). https://doi.org/10.1214/17-aoas1101, pp. 157, 159

Inference3

Standard errors, tests, and their validity.

MATCHING-INFER-01severity if flagged: highmedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: pass

A paper must not compute matching-estimator standard errors or confidence intervals from an individual-observation nonparametric bootstrap.

In plain wordsThe paper should not compute standard errors or confidence intervals for its matching estimate with an ordinary bootstrap that resamples individual observations.
Why it mattersThat bootstrap gives unreliable standard errors for simple matching estimators; valid options include resampling whole matched sets, the Abadie-Imbens variance formula, or clustering by matched set.
CheckWhether the paper's reported standard error / confidence interval for a matching estimator comes from an ordinary nonparametric bootstrap that resamples individual units.
PassThe paper does not use the individual-observation nonparametric bootstrap for its matching-estimator inference -- e.g. it uses the Abadie-Imbens variance estimator, a block bootstrap resampling matched pairs/sets, matched-set clustered errors, or is silent about using the bootstrap.
FlagThe paper states its matching-estimator standard errors or confidence intervals come from a standard nonparametric bootstrap resampling individual observations.
Further reading
  • Alberto Abadie & Guido W. Imbens. (2006). Large Sample Properties of Matching Estimators for Average Treatment Effects. Econometrica, 74(1), 235-267. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2006.00655.x, pp. 236, 252
  • Alberto Abadie & Jann Spiess. (2020). Robust Post-Matching Inference. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 117(538), 983-995. https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2020.1840383, p. 989
MATCHING-INFER-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: multi_continuous_matchingsilent: flag

A paper matching on two or more continuous covariates must report a bias-corrected estimator unless it documents a no-correction carve-out.

In plain wordsWhen the paper matches on two or more continuous covariates, it should report a bias-corrected matching estimate, unless it shows it matches on at most one continuous covariate or matches treated units to a much larger control pool.
Why it mattersSimple matching on several continuous covariates leaves a bias that does not shrink fast enough as the sample grows, which can distort the result.
CheckFor a paper matching on two or more continuous covariates, whether it reports a bias correction or falls into a stated no-correction carve-out.
PassThe paper reports a bias-adjusted / regression-corrected matching estimator, or documents that it matches on at most one continuous covariate, or that it matches only the treated to a control pool of much larger order.
FlagThe paper reports a simple, unadjusted matching estimate on two or more continuous covariates with no bias correction and no stated carve-out.
Further reading
  • Alberto Abadie & Guido W. Imbens. (2006). Large Sample Properties of Matching Estimators for Average Treatment Effects. Econometrica, 74(1), 235-267. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2006.00655.x, pp. 236, 252
  • Jasjeet S. Sekhon. (2011). Multivariate and Propensity Score Matching Software with Automated Balance Optimization: The<b>Matching</b>Package for<i>R</i>. Journal of Statistical Software, 42(7). https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v042.i07, p. 5 (PDF pagination)
MATCHING-INFER-03severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: interferencesilent: flag

A paper estimating effects under interference must use a conservative or dependence-aware variance estimator for its intervals.

In plain wordsWhen the paper estimates effects with units affecting each other, its confidence intervals should use a variance method that is cautious or accounts for that dependence, not one that assumes independent units.
Why it mattersSpillovers create dependence among units' exposures, so methods that ignore it make results look more precise than they are.
CheckFor a paper estimating effects under interference: whether its reported variance/confidence intervals come from a conservative or dependence-aware estimator rather than a naive OLS / difference-in-means variance that assumes independence.
PassThe paper uses a variance estimator that is conservative or accounts for the exposure dependence (e.g. a design-based conservative variance or clustering keyed to the exposure/dependency structure) when forming intervals under interference.
FlagThe paper reports intervals under interference from a naive OLS or difference-in-means variance that ignores the clustering interference induces in exposure conditions.
Further reading
  • Peter M. Aronow & Cyrus Samii. (2017). Estimating average causal effects under general interference, with application to a social network experiment. The Annals of Applied Statistics, 11(4). https://doi.org/10.1214/16-aoas1005, pp. 11, 24, 29 (PDF pagination)

Reporting2

What the paper must state for a reader to evaluate it.

MATCHING-REPORT-01severity if flagged: highhigh consensus, 6 sourcesapplies to: allsilent: flag

A matching/weighting paper must report a post-adjustment covariate-balance diagnostic on the covariates it adjusted on.

In plain wordsThe paper should report how similar treated and control groups are on the adjusted covariates after matching or weighting, such as a balance table or standardized mean differences.
Why it mattersMatching or weighting does not always improve balance and can even worsen it, so match quality must be checked in the actual sample.
CheckWhether the paper reports achieved covariate balance after matching/weighting for the covariates it adjusted on (a balance table, standardized differences, or comparable summary), located anywhere in the paper or its supplements.
PassThe paper reports a post-adjustment balance diagnostic covering its covariates -- e.g. treated-vs-control means or standardized mean differences before and after, or another distributional balance summary.
FlagThe paper reports a matched/weighted effect but shows no post-adjustment balance for the covariates it adjusted on (e.g. it reports only the propensity-model fit, or asserts balance without any diagnostic).
Further reading
  • Alberto Abadie & Jann Spiess. (2020). Robust Post-Matching Inference. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 117(538), 983-995. https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2020.1840383, p. 986
  • Stefano M. Iacus, Gary King, & Giuseppe Porro. (2011). Causal Inference without Balance Checking: Coarsened Exact Matching. Political Analysis, 20(1), 1-24. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpr013, p. 2
  • Gary King & Richard A. Nielsen. (2019). Why Propensity Scores Should Not Be Used for Matching. Political Analysis, 27(4), 435-454. https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2019.11, p. 451
  • Jasjeet S. Sekhon. (2011). Multivariate and Propensity Score Matching Software with Automated Balance Optimization: The<b>Matching</b>Package for<i>R</i>. Journal of Statistical Software, 42(7). https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v042.i07, p. 13 (PDF pagination)
MATCHING-REPORT-02severity if flagged: mediummedium consensus, 2 sourcesapplies to: weightingsilent: flag

A weighting paper should report a diagnostic of weight variability/stability (weight distribution or maximum, coefficient of variation, or effective sample size).

In plain wordsWhen the paper uses weights to estimate effects, it should report how variable the weights are, such as the largest weights, their coefficient of variation, or the effective sample size.
Why it mattersA few very large weights can dominate the estimate and inflate its uncertainty, so readers need to see the weight spread.
CheckFor a paper that estimates effects with weights: whether it reports a diagnostic of the weight distribution (largest weights, coefficient of variation of the weights, or effective sample size).
PassThe paper reports at least one weight-stability quantity -- e.g. the distribution or maximum of the weights, their coefficient of variation, or the effective sample size implied by the weights.
FlagThe paper reports weighted effect estimates but gives no diagnostic of how variable or concentrated the weights are.
Further reading
  • José R. Zubizarreta. (2015). Stable Weights that Balance Covariates for Estimation With Incomplete Outcome Data. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 110(511), 910-922. https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.2015.1023805, pp. 910, 912

Data1

Sample construction and measurement.

MATCHING-DATA-01severity if flagged: mediumlow consensus, 1 sourceapplies to: missing_datasilent: flag

A paper with missing matching-covariate values must state it used multiple imputation or another principled method rather than listwise deletion.

In plain wordsWhen matching covariates have missing values, the paper should state that it handled them with multiple imputation or another principled method rather than dropping incomplete cases.
Why it mattersSimply dropping cases with missing matching variables is not appropriate handling and can affect the result.
CheckFor a paper that reports missing values on matching covariates: whether it states it used multiple imputation or another proper missing-data method, rather than dropping incomplete cases.
PassThe paper states it handled the missing covariate values with multiple imputation or another principled approach.
FlagThe paper has missing matching-covariate values and either listwise-deletes the incomplete cases or says nothing about how the missingness was handled.
Further reading
  • Stefano M. Iacus, Gary King, & Giuseppe Porro. (2011). Causal Inference without Balance Checking: Coarsened Exact Matching. Political Analysis, 20(1), 1-24. https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpr013, p. 15
